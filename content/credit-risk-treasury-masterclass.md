## 1. The bank and the journey

A **balance sheet** records what the bank owns, what it owes and what belongs to its owners at a reporting date.

An **asset** is a resource or claim from which the bank expects value. A **liability** is an obligation to someone else. **Equity** is the owners’ remaining interest after liabilities.

$$\text{Assets}=\text{Liabilities}+\text{Equity}$$
Assets = resources and claims; liabilities = obligations; equity = the residual belonging to shareholders.

**Capital** includes money shareholders contributed. **Retained earnings** are profits kept in the bank rather than distributed. Both support the equity line; losses reduce that protection.

The **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** is India’s central bank and banking supervisor. Amounts below are in **Indian rupees (INR)**, expressed in ₹ crore.

| Assets | ₹ crore | Liabilities and equity | ₹ crore |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Cash and balances with RBI | 130 | Retail deposits | 900 |
| Investments | 520 | Wholesale deposits | 400 |
| Advances, net of allowance | 978 | Borrowings | 200 |
| Fixed and other assets | 72 | Other liabilities | 50 |
| — | — | Equity capital and reserves | 150 |
| **Total** | **1,700** | **Total** | **1,700** |

**Advances** are loans made by the bank. The underlying loan balance is ₹1,000 crore; the **loss allowance**, an accounting deduction for expected non-collection, is ₹22 crore. The asset shown is ₹978 crore.

The investment line contains ₹400 crore of government securities and ₹120 crore of corporate securities. A **security** is a financial claim that can be held and transferred.

**Deposits** are customer money the bank must repay. A **counterparty** is the other party in a financial relationship. Retail deposits come from individuals and households; wholesale deposits come from larger corporate and financial counterparties. **Borrowings** are funding raised through market or other borrowing arrangements.

The bank also has ₹108 crore of **undrawn commitments**: lending it has promised but has not yet paid out. These sit **off the balance sheet** until drawn, while already creating risk and funding obligations.

**Trap — allowance, capital and cash are different.** An allowance reduces an asset’s accounting value. Capital absorbs losses. Cash settles payments. Neither an allowance nor capital is a separate pot of cash.

The **banking book** contains lending, investment and funding positions managed as part of the continuing banking business. The **trading book** contains positions held for trading and market-making, where the bank buys and sells to serve customers or earn trading returns.

| Journey | Balance-sheet position | What is established |
|---|---|---|
| Hold and trade | Advances, investments and market contracts | Product, cash flows, currency and book |
| Measure asset risk | Loans, securities and commitments | Likelihood of failure, loss severity and exposure |
| Value and provide | Asset value and loss allowance | What is carried; what reduces profit |
| Protect the bank | Equity and qualifying capital instruments | Loss absorption relative to risk |
| Fund the assets | Deposits, borrowings and liquid assets | Cash availability, funding stability and rate mismatch |
| Price internally | The connection between assets and funding | Which business earns each part of the spread |
| Produce and prove | Records covering both sides | Reconciled calculations and submitted returns |
| Build and change | The systems carrying those records | Defined rules, controlled implementation and evidence |

A rupee is not physically labelled with its funding deposit. Its journey is an accounting and risk relationship: a product creates cash flows; those cash flows create exposure and funding needs; the same identified record supplies the calculations and the returns.

## 2. What the bank holds and trades

The advances and investment lines hold customer and issuer promises; deposits and borrowings fund them. Market contracts can add assets, liabilities or commitments around those positions.

### 2.1 Loans: principal, interest and repayment shape

In the banking-book advances line, **principal** is the amount lent and still contractually repayable. **Interest** is the charge for using that money.

A **cash flow** is a payment or receipt on a date. **Origination** is when the exposure is first created. **Maturity** is its final contractual repayment date; **tenor** is a stated length of time.

| Loan feature | Plain meaning | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed rate | Customer interest rate is fixed for the agreed period | Income cannot immediately follow market rates |
| Floating rate | Customer rate resets against a reference rate | Income changes at reset dates |
| Bullet repayment | Principal returns at maturity | Funding remains tied up until the end |
| Amortising repayment | Principal returns in instalments | Outstanding funding need reduces over time |
| Revolving facility | Repaid amounts may be drawn again within a limit | Today’s balance understates possible future use |
| Prepayment right | Borrower may repay before contractual maturity | Expected asset life can shorten |

A **benchmark** is the reference rate used to determine a floating rate. A **reset date** is when that rate is updated. A **spread** is the difference between two rates.

A **sanctioned limit** is the approved maximum facility amount. **Drawn** means already used; **undrawn** means the remaining available amount. **Utilisation** is the portion of the limit currently used.

**Trap — maturity and repricing are different dates.** A loan can require funding until final maturity while its interest rate resets much sooner. Funding duration and rate sensitivity must therefore be identified separately.

The same distinction follows the loan into loss measurement, liquidity buckets and internal pricing.

### 2.2 Bonds and the investment line

In the ₹520 crore investment line, a **bond** is a debt security: the issuer raises money and promises payments under transferable terms. The **issuer** is the party that owes those payments.

| Bond term | Meaning | What it fixes |
|---|---|---|
| Face value, or par | Contractual principal repaid at maturity | Final principal payment |
| Coupon | Interest paid on face value | Scheduled interest receipts |
| Maturity | Final repayment date | Contractual life |
| Issuer | Government, company or other borrower | Source of repayment risk |

A bond’s transferability can make it easier to sell than a private loan. It does not guarantee an active market or repayment.

| Instrument | Cash-flow feature | Balance-sheet use |
|---|---|---|
| Government security | Government debt; coupons and principal, or discount instruments | Investment, reserve holding and liquidity resource |
| Treasury bill | Short government debt issued below repayment value; no coupon | Short-term investment and liquidity |
| Corporate bond | Company debt with issuer repayment risk | Investment income and corporate exposure |
| Floating rate note | Coupon resets against a benchmark | Reduced sensitivity to movements in the benchmark rate |
| Zero coupon bond | All contractual value arrives at maturity | No intermediate coupon receipts |
| Covered or securitised instrument | Repayment supported by an asset pool structure | Requires the pool and structure to be identified |

**Securitisation** packages exposures into securities backed by their cash flows. Product identity must retain this structure; a generic “bond” label does not describe every repayment source.

**Trap — government debt can have low credit risk and material price risk.** The issuer’s ability to repay and today’s market value are separate questions.

### 2.3 Shares: ownership on the asset side

An equity holding in the investment or trading book is ownership in another company. It is different from the bank’s own equity on the liability-and-equity side.

A **share** gives a residual ownership claim. A **dividend** is a distribution to shareholders; it is not a contractual interest payment.

| Feature | Bond | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | Contractual debt payments | Residual ownership |
| Payment priority | Ahead of ordinary shareholders | After creditors |
| Maturity | Usually specified | None |
| Return | Contractual payments and price movement | Dividends and price movement |

**Market capitalisation** is share price multiplied by shares outstanding. **Dividend yield** is annual dividend divided by share price. The **price-to-earnings ratio** compares price with earnings per share.

**Beta** measures sensitivity to broad equity-market movement: how strongly the share tends to move with the market.

A **corporate action** changes shareholder entitlements or the form of a holding. Dividends, splits, bonus issues, rights issues and buybacks require dated position and price adjustments.

**Trap — a split changes share count and price, not total value merely by splitting.** Unadjusted data can manufacture a price loss.

Holdings in other financial institutions’ capital also require specific capital treatment. Owning another bank’s shares is not equivalent to holding cash that absorbs the bank’s own losses.

### 2.4 Currency positions

Foreign-currency assets and liabilities can sit in either book. **Foreign exchange (FX)** is the exchange of one currency for another.

A currency quotation states how much of one currency buys a unit of another. Its order matters: an INR price for a **United States dollar (USD)** rises when that dollar becomes more expensive in rupees.

**Long** means a position benefits from a rise in the relevant price; **short** means it benefits from a fall. An FX trade buys one currency and sells another.

| Transaction | Agreement | Cash timing |
|---|---|---|
| Spot | Exchange at the current agreed rate | Near-term settlement |
| Forward | Fix today the rate for a future exchange | Agreed future date |
| FX swap | Exchange currencies, then reverse the exchange later | Two linked settlement dates |

An FX swap can manage funding in one currency against resources in another. Its near and far legs must remain linked in the cash-flow record.

A forward rate reflects the interest-rate difference between the currencies under **covered interest parity**: the relationship that aligns returns when the exchange rate is fixed in advance.

$$F=S\times\frac{1+r_{\mathrm{INR}}}{1+r_{\mathrm{USD}}}$$
F = forward INR price of one USD; S = spot price in the same quotation; rINR and rUSD = interest rates for the same single period in INR and USD.

**Trap — a forward rate is not a forecast of future spot.** Interest-rate differences help determine the agreed rate. A forward position still has currency and interest-rate sensitivities.

The **net open position (NOP)** is the currency exposure remaining after combining relevant assets, liabilities and market contracts. Omitting one product system can understate the bank’s currency risk.

### 2.5 Derivatives around the balance sheet

A derivative can hedge a banking-book asset or funding position, or form part of trading activity. A **derivative** is a contract whose value depends on an underlying rate, price or index.

**Hedging** means taking an offsetting position against an existing risk. **Speculation** means deliberately taking a market exposure. The contract alone does not establish which purpose applies.

**Over the counter (OTC)** means agreed directly between counterparties rather than as an exchange-standardised trade.

| Contract | Rights and obligations | Operational character |
|---|---|---|
| Forward | Both parties commit to a future transaction | Usually customised and OTC |
| Future | Both parties commit under standardised terms | Exchange trading and daily margin |
| Swap | Parties exchange specified payment streams | Often OTC; may be centrally cleared |
| Option | Buyer has a right; seller must perform if exercised | Buyer pays a premium |

A **notional** is the reference amount used to calculate contractual payments. In a plain interest rate swap, the notional principal is not itself exchanged.

An **interest rate swap** exchanges fixed and floating interest streams. Paying fixed and receiving floating can offset floating funding costs behind fixed-rate assets. The match depends on amounts, reset dates and benchmarks.

The **Mumbai Interbank Offered Rate (MIBOR)** is an interest-rate benchmark used in floating-rate contracts.

An option’s **strike** is the agreed exercise price; **expiry** is when the right ends; **premium** is its purchase price. A **call** gives the right to buy; a **put** gives the right to sell.

“In the money” means exercise has positive intrinsic value. It does not mean the buyer has recovered the premium.

A **cap** pays when a reference interest rate exceeds its strike; a **floor** pays when it falls below. A **swaption** gives the right to enter a swap.

**Trap — option buyer and seller have different loss shapes.** A purchased option’s loss is limited to its premium under its contractual payoff. An uncovered seller can face much larger losses; an uncovered call can have unlimited upside exposure.

### 2.6 The book boundary and the accounting classification

For the same security or derivative, the book designation places risk in the banking or trading framework. Accounting classification establishes how value changes enter the accounts. Both fields must be governed and consistent with the instrument’s purpose.

**Fair value** is a current market-based value. **Amortised cost** updates the starting accounting amount for interest and cash repayments, with an allowance for expected credit loss. **Profit and loss (P&L)** records income and expenses for a period.

**Other comprehensive income (OCI)** records specified gains and losses outside current P&L, within equity.

| Dimension | Banking book | Trading book |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Continuing lending, investment and funding | Trading and market-making |
| Main rate-risk view | Earnings and lifetime balance-sheet value | Daily market sensitivities and trading losses |
| Accounting route | Depends on accounting category; can include amortised cost or fair value | Trading positions generally pass fair-value changes through P&L |
| Reporting destination | Credit, liquidity and banking-book risk returns | Market-risk and trading returns, with other applicable exposure reporting |

**Fair value through profit and loss (FVPL)** puts fair-value changes into P&L. **Fair value through other comprehensive income (FVOCI)** directs specified fair-value changes into OCI.

**Trap — the book flag is not a complete accounting rule.** A banking-book hedge can still be measured at fair value. Book, accounting category and hedge designation are related fields, not interchangeable labels.

**Hedge accounting** is accounting treatment for a documented qualifying hedge relationship. Without the required designation and evidence, an effective economic hedge can still produce accounting volatility because its two sides use different measurement bases.

Moving a position merely to obtain favourable accounting or capital treatment is **regulatory arbitrage**. Designation, authorised transfers and their capital consequences need an audit trail.

The boundary determines the risk framework and reporting route; the associated accounting classification determines the valuation basis. A bad book mapping can therefore send one instrument to the wrong measure, the wrong accounting treatment and the wrong return.

## 3. What can go wrong on the asset side

In banking-book advances and debt investments, the bank expects someone else to pay. Commitments can become additional assets when drawn. Trading contracts add exposures whose size can change with markets.

### 3.1 Default: the event being measured

For a loan or debt security, **credit risk** is the risk that promised payments are not received in full or on time.

An **obligor** is the borrower responsible for repayment. A **facility** is an individual lending arrangement with that obligor. One obligor can have several facilities.

**Default** is the event recognised when payment failure or evidence of inability to repay meets the applicable definition.

**Days past due (DPD)** measures elapsed days since a contractual payment became overdue.

| Default limb | What it establishes | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Payment delinquency | A material obligation passes the applicable overdue threshold | Due dates, unpaid amounts and payment records |
| Unlikely to pay | Full repayment is unlikely without recovery action | Financial distress, restructuring evidence or other approved indicators |

**Trap — waiting for an overdue threshold can recognise distress too late.** The inability-to-pay assessment can establish default before the mechanical backstop.

The definition needs an approved policy, consistent application and retained evidence. Default history feeds model estimation, so inconsistent recognition contaminates future probabilities as well as today’s classification.

Borrower-level classification and facility-level payment data must remain distinguishable. An obligor-wide default rule does not justify overwriting the actual payment history of each facility.

### 3.2 The three credit-loss inputs

On each advances or commitment record, the loss question separates into event likelihood, amount exposed and loss severity.

| Input | Plain-English definition | Main driver |
|---|---|---|
| **Probability of default (PD)** | Chance of default within a stated horizon | Borrower credit quality |
| **Loss given default (LGD)** | Fraction of exposure lost if default occurs | Recoveries, costs and timing |
| **Exposure at default (EAD)** | Amount exposed when default occurs | Drawn balance, future drawings and repayments |

**Expected loss (EL)** is the probability-weighted average credit loss for a defined measurement period and basis.

$$EL=PD\times LGD\times EAD$$
PD = default probability; LGD = loss fraction conditional on default; EAD = exposure amount at default. All inputs must use compatible horizons and definitions.

PD is a probability. LGD is a proportion. EAD is money. Their product is money.

**Trap — security does not make a weak borrower unlikely to fail.** It can improve recovery after failure. A lower LGD is not automatically a lower PD.

### 3.3 Ratings and how probability changes through time

Within advances, the **borrower rating** grades the obligor’s credit quality and supports PD. A **facility rating** assesses transaction features such as security and repayment priority, supporting loss-severity assessment.

**Seniority** is the contractual or legal order in which claims are paid. Two facilities to the same borrower can share PD and have different LGDs.

**Migration** is movement between rating grades. A **downgrade** means worsening credit quality; an **upgrade** means improvement.

A **transition matrix** records the probability of moving from each starting grade to each ending grade over a specified period.

| Matrix feature | Meaning | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Starting-grade row | Borrowers beginning in one grade | Population and starting date defined |
| Ending-grade columns | Possible states at the horizon | Grades and default state exhaustive |
| Default column | Probability of default over that interval | Consistent default definition |
| Row total | Probability of ending somewhere | Must total 100% |

An **absorbing state** is a model state from which probability does not leave. Treating default as absorbing counts whether default has occurred; it does not assert that recoveries or cures are impossible.

Applying a transition matrix through successive periods produces a **PD term structure**: default probabilities across future horizons.

Two assumptions require challenge. **Markov** means the current grade alone determines the next transition, ignoring the path into that grade. **Time-homogeneous** means the same transition probabilities apply in every period. Downgrade momentum and changing economic conditions can make both inadequate.

The four time-related quantities must remain separate.

| Quantity | Definition | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Cumulative PD | Probability of default by a date | Total default probability over a horizon |
| Survival probability | Probability of no default by a date | Remaining performing population |
| Marginal PD | Probability, viewed today, of default during one future period | Period-specific expected loss |
| Conditional PD | Default probability during a period, given survival to its start | Default risk among survivors |

$$S(t)=1-F(t)$$
S(t) = survival probability to time t; F(t) = cumulative PD to time t.

$$m(t)=F(t)-F(t-1)$$
m(t) = marginal PD during period t; F(t) and F(t−1) = cumulative PDs at the period’s end and start.

$$q(t)=\frac{m(t)}{S(t-1)}$$
q(t) = conditional PD during period t; m(t) = marginal PD; S(t−1) = probability of surviving to the period’s start.

Marginal PDs across the remaining periods plus final survival must total one. Cumulative PD must not decline.

**Trap — do not sum cumulative PDs as period losses.** Earlier defaults would be counted repeatedly. Conditional PD must be multiplied by survival to become a probability viewed from today.

The same borrower can legitimately have different PD estimates for different purposes.

| Basis | What it reflects | Main application |
|---|---|---|
| **Point-in-time (PIT)** | Current borrower and economic conditions | Forward-looking accounting loss estimates |
| **Through-the-cycle (TTC)** | Credit performance across good and bad periods | Long-run capital calibration |

Each estimate needs a purpose, horizon, model version and effective date. A field called “PD” without those attributes is incomplete.

### 3.4 Recovery severity

On a defaulted advances record, repayment may come from the borrower, collateral, a guarantor or a resolution process. **Collateral** is an asset supporting repayment. A **guarantor** is a third party promising to meet specified obligations.

A recovery received later is worth less today. **Discounting** converts future money to its value at a chosen measurement date; the result is **present value (PV)**.

$$LGD=1-\frac{PV(\text{net recoveries})}{EAD}$$
PV(net recoveries) = recovery cash flows after relevant costs, discounted to the measurement date; EAD = exposure on the compatible basis.

**Net recoveries** subtract legal, valuation, enforcement and sale costs. Expected collection timing is therefore as important as estimated sale proceeds.

| Change, other inputs fixed | LGD direction | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Higher realisable recovery | Falls | More of the exposure returns |
| Higher enforcement cost | Rises | Less cash remains |
| Longer recovery delay | Rises | Recovery has lower present value |
| Weaker collateral market | Rises | Sale proceeds decline |

**Downturn LGD** measures loss severity under adverse recovery conditions. It is relevant because borrower distress and collateral weakness can occur together.

**Trap — a collateral valuation is not a recovery estimate.** Enforceability, saleability, costs, priority and delay stand between valuation and collected cash.

Accounting and capital can require different LGD calibrations. A shared collateral record does not mean one unlabelled LGD is appropriate for both.

### 3.5 Exposure before and after drawing

In advances, today’s balance is already funded. On an undrawn commitment, exposure can grow before the borrower defaults.

A **credit conversion factor (CCF)** estimates or prescribes the share of undrawn availability included in exposure under a specified framework.

$$EAD=\text{Drawn exposure}+CCF\times\text{Undrawn amount}$$
Drawn exposure = funded amount on the measurement basis; CCF = conversion fraction; undrawn amount = available commitment not yet used.

A borrower approaching distress may use remaining headroom. Ignoring that use understates loss exposure and the liquidity needed to honour the commitment.

An amortising facility can move in the opposite direction: scheduled principal repayment reduces future exposure, subject to the relevant repayment assumptions.

**Trap — the credit CCF and the liquidity drawdown factor are not the same parameter.** Capital, accounting loss and stressed cash outflow calculations answer different questions. Retain separate rule references even when they consume the same limit and utilisation record.

### 3.6 Mitigation: which part of the exposure changes

For secured advances and collateralised market contracts, **credit risk mitigation** is recognised protection that changes the relevant exposure or loss calculation.

| Protection | Mechanism | Measurement effect |
|---|---|---|
| Eligible financial collateral | Recognised liquid assets support the claim | Can reduce adjusted exposure |
| Guarantee or credit protection | Eligible third party covers specified obligations | Can substitute covered credit treatment |
| Enforceable netting | Permitted claims and obligations offset | Reduces recognised net exposure |
| Physical collateral | Property, plant or other assets support recovery | Influences LGD; capital recognition depends on approach |

**Netting** is the legally effective offset of obligations. Merely adding positive and negative balances does not establish a right to net in insolvency.

Protection requires documentation, required registration or perfection, current valuation and monitoring. **Perfection** means completing legal steps needed to establish the security interest and its priority.

A **haircut** is an adjustment reducing recognised value, or increasing measured exposure, for possible adverse movement.

| Adjustment | Risk addressed | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Exposure haircut | Exposure can rise before close-out | Adjusted exposure increases |
| Collateral haircut | Collateral can lose value | Recognised collateral decreases |
| Currency mismatch | Protection and exposure use different currencies | Protection recognition reduces |
| Maturity mismatch | Protection expires before the exposure | Coverage recognition reduces |

**Trap — do not subtract every kind of “security value” from EAD.** Financial collateral, physical collateral, guarantees and netting use different recognition mechanics.

### 3.7 The portfolio and the loss beyond the average

Across the whole advances and investment book, **concentration** means too much exposure shares a borrower, group, sector, geography or economic driver. **Correlation** means outcomes tend to move together.

| Concentration | What clusters | Why single-facility analysis misses it |
|---|---|---|
| Single name or connected group | Large exposure to one repayment source | Each facility can appear acceptable separately |
| Sector | Borrowers dependent on similar demand or costs | Different names share the same business pressure |
| Geography or common factor | Exposure to a region, currency or commodity | Legal separation does not remove shared drivers |

Portfolio EL is the sum of individual ELs on consistent bases. That addition does **not** require independence. Correlation matters particularly to the distribution of aggregate losses and its adverse tail.

**Unexpected loss (UL)** is loss beyond the expected level, measured through the distribution of possible outcomes.

| Dimension | EL | UL |
|---|---|---|
| Describes | Probability-weighted average loss | Adverse deviation beyond the average |
| Main response | Pricing and loss allowances | Capital and portfolio limits |
| Depends heavily on | PD, LGD and EAD | Those inputs plus co-movement and concentration |
| Common confusion | An allowance is not an annual cash saving | Capital is not a guarantee against every possible loss |

The **Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI)** measures concentration by adding squared exposure shares.

$$HHI=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i^2$$
wi = exposure i divided by total exposure; n = number of exposures included.

$$\text{Effective equal-sized names}=\frac{1}{HHI}$$
HHI = the concentration index calculated from the chosen exposure shares.

Larger positions contribute disproportionately because shares are squared. More uneven exposure raises HHI and reduces the effective number of equal-sized names.

**Trap — HHI measures size concentration, not all correlation.** A collection of equally sized borrowers can still depend on the same economic factor.

Large-exposure controls therefore aggregate connected counterparties and compare exposure with eligible capital. Their limit basis must not be confused with exposure as a percentage of the loan book.

Asset risk is now described in three dimensions: what each borrower may lose, how market contracts change the exposure, and how losses can arrive together. Valuation converts those risks into carrying amounts and charges against profit.

## 4. What the assets are worth and what is provided against them

In advances and investments, contractual claims become accounting values. For derivatives, the current value can sit on either side of the balance sheet. The measurement basis determines which changes affect P&L, OCI or the loss allowance.

### 4.1 The loan’s effective yield and carrying amount

For an amortised-cost loan in advances, the contractual principal and the initial accounting investment can differ because of fees integral to the lending arrangement.

The **effective interest rate (EIR)** is the rate that makes the estimated contractual receipts equal the initial net investment. It incorporates relevant fees into the yield over the exposure’s life.

$$I_0=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{CF_t}{(1+EIR)^t}$$
I0 = initial net investment; CFt = estimated receipt at time t; n = final period; EIR = effective rate per period; t = time measured in matching periods.

**Trap — a fee integral to the loan’s yield is not automatically day-one fee income.** It is released through the effective-interest calculation.

The **gross carrying amount (GCA)** is the amortised-cost balance before deducting the loss allowance.

$$GCA_t=GCA_{t-1}\times(1+EIR)-\text{Cash received}_t$$
GCAt−1 = opening gross carrying amount; EIR = effective rate for the period; cash receivedt = payments reducing the balance during the period; GCAt = closing gross amount under the stated roll-forward.

This simple roll-forward assumes unchanged terms and the stated payment timing. Modifications, further lending and write-offs need their own movement lines.

**Amortisation** here means releasing a fee, discount or premium through the effective yield. It is distinct from a borrower’s principal-amortisation schedule.

The **net carrying amount** is the asset after the allowance.

$$\text{Net carrying amount}=GCA-\text{Loss allowance}$$
GCA = value before the credit-loss deduction; loss allowance = recognised estimate of non-collection.

| Amount | What it represents | What moves it |
|---|---|---|
| Contractual principal | Principal legally outstanding | Drawings and principal repayments |
| GCA | Accounting amount before allowance | Effective interest, cash receipts and relevant accounting events |
| Loss allowance | Expected credit shortfall | Credit deterioration, recoveries and measurement assumptions |
| Net carrying amount | Asset after allowance | Changes in GCA and allowance |

**Trap — a downgrade alone does not mark an amortised-cost loan down to market.** Deterioration is reflected through the allowance. A modification or write-off is a separate event.

For the fixed-rate loan framework, credit deterioration does not replace the original EIR with today’s higher market yield. Otherwise the original lending bargain and the subsequent credit loss would become mixed.

### 4.2 Market value, price and yield

On the investment line, a bond’s current value discounts its remaining payments at the return required by the market.

**Yield to maturity (YTM)** is the single rate equating a bond’s contractual future cash flows with its current price. The arithmetic resembles EIR, but the valuation date and purpose differ.

$$P=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{CF_t}{(1+y)^t}$$
P = bond price on the stated cash-flow basis; CFt = coupon or principal cash flow at time t; y = yield per matching period; n = final period.

| Comparison | EIR | Current YTM |
|---|---|---|
| Starting value | Initial accounting investment | Current bond price |
| Main use | Effective-interest accounting | Market quotation and valuation |
| Effect of market repricing | Does not itself replace the original fixed-rate EIR | Changes with price |
| Shared idea | Discounts receipts to a value | Discounts receipts to a value |

For a conventional fixed-coupon bond, **yield rises → price falls**. **Yield falls → price rises**. The coupon has not changed; the price adjusts to the return now required.

A **premium** means price above par; a **discount** means price below par. On a conventional bond, yield below coupon corresponds to a premium, and yield above coupon to a discount.

**Accrued interest** is coupon interest earned since the last payment date. **Clean price** excludes it; **dirty price** includes it.

$$\text{Dirty price}=\text{Clean price}+\text{Accrued interest}$$
Dirty price = settlement price including accrual; clean price = quoted price excluding accrual; accrued interest = earned coupon not yet paid.

**Trap — quote clean, settle dirty.** A valuation-to-ledger difference can be an accrual-basis mismatch rather than a market-price error.

### 4.3 Duration, spread and market-loss measures

In banking- and trading-book securities, **market risk** is the risk of adverse value movements caused by rates, prices, spreads or currencies.

**Macaulay duration** is the average time to receive cash flows, weighted by their present values. **Modified duration** translates that timing into approximate price sensitivity to yield.

$$D_{\mathrm{mod}}=\frac{D_{\mathrm{Mac}}}{1+y}$$
Dmod = modified duration; DMac = Macaulay duration; y = annual yield under the annual-payment convention shown.

$$\frac{\Delta P}{P}\approx-D_{\mathrm{mod}}\times\Delta y$$
ΔP = price change; P = starting price; Dmod = modified duration; Δy = yield change expressed as a decimal.

Coupon frequency and yield convention must match the calculation. The annual formula must not be applied unchanged to a different compounding convention.

| Feature, other inputs comparable | Rate-sensitivity direction | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| More cash received later | Usually increases | More value depends on distant discounting |
| Earlier principal repayment | Reduces | Less value remains tied up |
| Floating coupon resetting to market | Reduces benchmark-rate duration | Future coupon income adjusts |
| No coupons before maturity | Concentrates timing at maturity | All cash arrives at the end |

**Trap — a floating-rate instrument does not have zero risk.** Reset timing, credit spread and other contractual features still matter. A zero coupon bond’s Macaulay duration equals maturity; that statement does not make modified duration equal maturity.

**Convexity** is curvature in the price–yield relationship. Duration is a local approximation. For a conventional option-free bond, convexity explains why the actual gain on a yield fall can exceed the straight-line estimate and the loss on a rise can be smaller.

A **basis point (bp)** is one hundredth of a percentage point.

**Dollar value of one basis point (DV01)** is the conventional name for the money value of a one-basis-point yield move. An INR portfolio’s DV01 is reported in rupees, often ₹ lakh per bp.

$$DV01=P\times D_{\mathrm{mod}}\times0.0001$$
P = position market value; Dmod = modified duration; 0.0001 = one bp expressed as a decimal yield change.

This formula gives a magnitude. A long conventional bond loses approximately that amount when yield rises by one bp; the reporting system must state its sign convention.

A **credit spread** is the yield increment over the relevant government benchmark for a corporate debt instrument.

$$\text{Corporate yield}=\text{Government benchmark yield}+\text{Credit spread}$$
The yields refer to comparable maturity and quotation bases; credit spread = the difference between them.

**Spread widens → bond value falls**, other inputs unchanged. A corporate bond can therefore lose value while government yields remain unchanged. Spread movement is a live credit signal, but it is not itself a PD.

| Sensitivity | Measures | Main application |
|---|---|---|
| DV01 | Money change per bp of yield | Bonds and rate positions |
| Delta | Value change per unit move in the underlying | Shares, FX and options |
| Vega | Value change with volatility | Options |
| Credit-spread sensitivity | Money change per bp of spread | Corporate debt |

**Volatility** measures the variability of market movements. **Value at risk (VaR)** is a modelled loss threshold for a specified horizon and confidence level.

$$VaR\approx z\times DV01\times\sigma_y$$
z = normal-distribution multiplier for the chosen confidence; DV01 = money sensitivity per bp; σy = yield volatility in bp over the same horizon.

The formula uses a linear position approximation and a stated distribution assumption. It is not a universal valuation method for options or large market moves.

**Expected shortfall (ES)** is the average modelled loss beyond the chosen tail threshold.

**Trap — VaR is not the maximum possible loss.** It says where a threshold lies, not how severe losses beyond it become. Stress testing examines adverse conditions that a single statistical threshold may conceal.

### 4.4 Derivative value, credit exposure and collateral cash

Around the asset and funding positions, a derivative’s **mark-to-market** is its current fair value. A positive value is a claim; a negative value is an obligation.

**Counterparty credit risk** is the risk that the other contracting party fails before meeting obligations.

| Quantity | Meaning | Main use |
|---|---|---|
| Notional | Payment-calculation reference amount | Contract specification |
| Market value | Current value of the contract | Accounting asset or liability |
| Current exposure | Positive replacement claim, subject to recognised offsets | Loss exposure if the counterparty fails now |
| **Potential future exposure (PFE)** | Possible future increase in positive exposure | Forward-looking counterparty measurement |

Derivative EAD incorporates current exposure and an allowance for future exposure under the applicable method. It is not obtained by treating the gross notional as a loan balance.

**Trap — market value can be negative; credit exposure is not a negative loss asset.** A liability to the counterparty does not create a negative standalone EAD.

A **credit support annex (CSA)** specifies collateral arrangements for a derivative relationship. **Variation margin** responds to current market-value changes. **Initial margin** protects against potential changes during the relevant close-out period.

**Central clearing** places a central counterparty between the original trading parties. The **Clearing Corporation of India Limited (CCIL)** performs central clearing functions in Indian markets. **Novation** replaces the original contract with contracts against the central counterparty.

Clearing, margin and enforceable netting reduce exposures through defined mechanisms. They do not make credit or liquidity risk disappear.

**Credit valuation adjustment (CVA)** adjusts derivative value for counterparty credit risk. Capital treatment for CVA risk is related to, but distinct from, the accounting adjustment.

**Wrong-way risk** occurs when exposure grows as the counterparty’s ability to pay weakens. It connects derivatives to the same recovery problem seen in credit: protection can weaken at the moment it is needed.

**Settlement risk** arises after the bank has paid its leg but before receiving the other. **Payment versus payment** makes the two currency payments conditional on one another.

The hedge therefore crosses into funding risk: it can reduce rate exposure while creating margin payments, settlement demands and a claim on another institution.

### 4.5 Expected credit loss and the three stages

In amortised-cost advances, the allowance records the expected shortfall. **Expected credit loss (ECL)** is a probability-weighted estimate of credit-related cash shortfalls, discounted to the reporting date.

**Indian Accounting Standard (Ind AS) 109** sets out expected-credit-loss accounting. The applicable accounting regime must be identified for the entity and reporting purpose; it must not be inferred merely from the fact that the business operates in India.

**Significant increase in credit risk (SICR)** means credit risk has risen materially since initial recognition.

| Stage | Credit condition | Allowance horizon | Interest-revenue basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stage 1 | No SICR since origination | Defaults possible within the next 12 months | EIR on GCA |
| Stage 2 | SICR, but not credit-impaired | Defaults over remaining life | EIR on GCA |
| Stage 3 | Credit-impaired | Expected cash shortfall over remaining life | EIR on net carrying amount |

**Credit-impaired** means events have adversely affected expected future cash flows. Staging can improve when the required cure conditions are met; the evidence must support the move.

**Trap — 12-month ECL is not cash lost during the next 12 months.** It includes the losses associated with defaults occurring in that window, even when recoveries and shortfalls extend beyond it.

A **discount factor (DF)** is the multiplier that converts a future amount into present value. A simplified single-period loss calculation is:

$$ECL=PD\times LGD\times EAD\times DF$$
PD = probability of default in the selected period; LGD = loss fraction conditional on default; EAD = exposure at that default time; DF = discount factor bringing the resulting loss to the reporting date.

$$DF_t=\frac{1}{(1+EIR)^t}$$
DFt = discount factor for time t; EIR = effective rate matching the time convention; t = time from the reporting date.

For a performing exposure measured over several periods:

$$ECL=\sum_{t=1}^{T}m(t)\times LGD_t\times EAD_t\times DF_t$$
T = remaining measurement horizon; m(t) = marginal PD in period t; LGDt = loss severity for default in that period; EADt = exposure then; DFt = discount to the reporting date.

LGD may already include the delay between default and recovery. The final DF then brings a default-date loss back to the reporting date. **Do not discount the same interval twice.**

### 4.6 The rating-to-profit connection

Within advances, a deterioration in the borrower rating changes the PD assessment. The comparison with origination determines whether SICR is present; the resulting stage determines the allowance horizon.

| SICR evidence | What is compared or observed | Data dependency |
|---|---|---|
| Quantitative | Current lifetime default risk against the comparable origination expectation | Retained origination and current risk measures |
| Qualitative | Watch-list status, covenant breach, forbearance or adverse developments | Dated credit-monitoring evidence |
| Overdue backstop | More than 30 DPD creates a rebuttable presumption | Reliable due dates and arrears |

A **covenant** is a contractual condition the borrower must meet. **Forbearance** is a concession granted because the borrower is in financial difficulty. A **rebuttable presumption** applies unless sufficient evidence supports an approved rebuttal.

**Trap — an absolute PD threshold is not the whole SICR test.** A borrower can have a relatively high but unchanged risk level, while a safer borrower has deteriorated significantly from its own starting point.

The chain is therefore: **rating deterioration → revised PD and other evidence → SICR assessment → stage and allowance horizon → allowance movement → P&L charge**.

Neither a rating nor PD mechanically determines stage alone. Qualitative evidence and credit impairment can determine the outcome before a model catches up.

Moving from Stage 1 to Stage 2 expands the default horizon from 12 months to remaining life. PD and LGD may also worsen. The horizon effect and parameter effects must be separated in the allowance movement.

### 4.7 Forward-looking information and overlays

Across the advances portfolio, ECL uses a range of possible economic outcomes. **Scenario weighting** assigns probabilities to those outcomes rather than relying only on one central view.

$$ECL_{\mathrm{weighted}}=\sum_{s=1}^{S}p_s\times ECL_s$$
ps = probability assigned to outcome s; ECLs = loss estimate under that outcome; S = number of outcomes; the probabilities sum to one.

Calculate internally consistent losses within each outcome, then weight them. Averaging inputs first need not reproduce the same result because the relationships can be nonlinear.

**Trap — weighted ECL need not equal the central estimate.** The adverse and favourable changes need not cancel.

A **management overlay** is a controlled adjustment for a risk or limitation not adequately captured in the model output.

An overlay needs a documented trigger, quantification method, owner, approval and release condition. Its relationship to existing parameter adjustments must be explicit.

**Trap — an overlay embedded in parameters and added again after the model is double counted.** An overlay with no release condition can become a permanent substitute for correcting the model.

Economic assumptions across impairment, stress testing and capital planning need to be consistent or their differences explicitly justified.

### 4.8 Credit-impaired value and the allowance walk

On a Stage 3 advances record, the focus is the gap between the gross claim and expected collections.

$$ECL=GCA-PV(\text{Expected net collections})$$
GCA = gross carrying amount; PV(expected net collections) = expected borrower and recovery cash flows after costs, discounted on the applicable EIR basis.

The net carrying amount then represents the present value of expected collections under this measurement.

**Trap — the Stage 3 interest switch changes the revenue base, not the identity of the original discount rate.** Interest on net value and discounting recovery cash flows are two different uses of the rate.

A **provision** is a recognised loss amount; the balance held against the asset is the allowance. An **impairment charge** is the amount recognised as an expense in P&L during the period.

The **allowance walk** explains how the opening allowance becomes the closing allowance.

| Movement | What it captures | Control |
|---|---|---|
| New lending and exposure changes | Added, repaid or removed exposure | Tie to the exposure population |
| Stage transfers | Change of measurement horizon or impairment status | Retain trigger and transfer evidence |
| Parameter remeasurement | PD, LGD, EAD and recovery revisions | Retain model and input versions |
| Overlays | Approved adjustment outside the base model | Identify addition, use and release |
| Write-offs and other movements | Allowance used or moved outside the ordinary expense flow | Separate from remeasurement expense |

A **write-off** removes an amount from the carrying balance when the applicable conditions are met. Using an existing allowance in a write-off is not automatically a fresh expense of the same amount.

$$\text{Closing allowance}=\text{Opening allowance}+\text{P\&L charge}+\text{Other net movements}$$
P&L charge = net impairment expense or reversal; other net movements = signed movements such as allowance used in write-offs and relevant non-P&L changes.

**Trap — closing allowance minus opening allowance equals the charge only when other movements are absent.** A stock of allowance and a flow of expense must not be confused.

A complete walk explains both the balance and the expense. Attribution order must be consistent where stage, parameter and exposure changes interact.

### 4.9 The same loan under Indian prudential classification

On the Indian advances line, **income recognition, asset classification and provisioning (IRAC)** rules govern prudential recognition and classification. They answer a different question from accounting ECL.

A **non-performing asset (NPA)** is an exposure classified as non-performing under the applicable prudential rules. A **special mention account (SMA)** is an early-warning category for stress before NPA recognition.

| Ordinary overdue ladder | Prudential status | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 30 DPD | Standard; SMA-0 where applicable | Early overdue stress |
| 31–60 DPD | Standard; SMA-1 | Further deterioration |
| 61–90 DPD | Standard; SMA-2 | Near the overdue NPA threshold |
| Over 90 DPD | NPA, initially sub-standard | Non-performance under the ordinary overdue test |
| Continued sub-standard status | Doubtful under the applicable ageing rule | Prolonged non-performance |
| Identified loss awaiting write-off | Loss asset | Loss recognised in substance |

The relevant product and classification rules determine application. A payment record, a borrower classification and an accounting stage must each retain their own basis.

| Dimension | ECL | IRAC provisioning |
|---|---|---|
| Main driver | Expected cash shortfall and deterioration since origination | Classification, ageing, security and prescribed treatment |
| Measurement | Forward-looking estimate | Prescribed rates on the relevant outstanding basis |
| Performing deterioration | Can trigger lifetime allowance before default | Can remain Standard while carrying an SMA flag |
| Interest after impairment | EIR on net amount in Stage 3 | NPA income recognition follows cash-receipt rules |

**Trap — Standard does not necessarily mean Stage 1.** A Standard exposure can already have SICR. Equally, the two allowance amounts do not have to match.

Provision rates are differentiated by classification, secured status and other applicable categories. Doubtful-asset treatment escalates with ageing; unsecured portions and identified losses receive more severe treatment.

Keep the rate table with its effective date, entity scope and regulatory reference. An illustrative percentage is not a production rule.

Where an applicable prudential floor exceeds the accounting allowance, an **impairment reserve** records the required appropriation under that regime. It must remain distinct from the ECL expense and allowance.

### 4.10 Shared-borrower information and resolution

For an advances borrower with multiple lenders, another lender’s classification is relevant information about the same repayment source.

The **Central Repository of Information on Large Credits (CRILC)** supports reporting and sharing of large-borrower credit information, including classification and stress status.

CRILC has two directions: the bank submits its exposure information, and incoming information supports monitoring. Thresholds, populations and reporting schedules belong to the applicable reporting specification.

**Restructuring** changes contractual terms, often because the borrower cannot meet the existing arrangement.

**Derecognition** removes the original financial asset from the accounts. Whether modified terms require derecognition is a governed accounting assessment.

| Outcome | Carrying-amount treatment | Yield treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Original asset continues | Recompute the modified cash flows and recognise the relevant modification effect | Retain original EIR for that measurement |
| Original asset derecognised | Remove original asset; recognise the new asset on the applicable basis | Establish the relevant new yield |
| Borrower remains impaired | Preserve the impairment evidence | A new contract does not establish credit recovery |

**Trap — do not turn a numerical modification test into an automatic universal rule for loan-asset derecognition.** The applicable accounting policy and qualitative assessment must govern the decision.

A **modification loss** is the reduction recognised when continuing-asset cash flows become less valuable under the required measurement.

Indian recovery routes include negotiated resolution under the prudential framework; security enforcement under the **Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act (SARFAESI)**; claims through the **Debt Recovery Tribunal (DRT)**; and corporate resolution under the **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC)**.

The legal route affects recovery cost, priority and timing. Those are LGD inputs, so a resolution delay can increase the allowance without any further principal being advanced.

The allowance now connects the asset to the other side of the sheet: an impairment expense reduces profit and retained earnings. That is where the capital effect begins.

## 5. The capital behind the assets

The equity and qualifying capital instruments on the funding side absorb losses arising from advances, investments and trading activity. Capital adequacy compares that protection with measured risk.

### 5.1 The capital stack

Within equity and borrowings, **regulatory capital** is the set of instruments and reserves recognised as loss-absorbing under the capital rules, after required adjustments.

| Component | What it contains | Loss-absorption character |
|---|---|---|
| **Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1)** | Eligible ordinary equity and retained earnings, after deductions | Primary ongoing loss absorption |
| **Additional Tier 1 (AT1)** | Qualifying perpetual capital instruments | Loss absorption through specified contractual or regulatory mechanisms |
| Tier 2 | Qualifying subordinated instruments | Further protection under the applicable loss-absorption framework |

**Subordinated** means ranking behind more senior creditors. Accounting equity and regulatory capital therefore overlap without being identical.

The bank’s ₹40 crore of Tier 2 borrowing is included within its ₹200 crore borrowing line. Its regulatory capital treatment does not remove the borrowing from the accounting balance sheet.

Deductions remove items that do not provide dependable loss absorption. **Goodwill** is the acquisition value above identifiable net assets; a **deferred tax asset** is a recognised future tax benefit. Their capital treatment reflects whether the value remains usable under stress.

**Trap — reserve balances at RBI are cash assets; retained-earnings reserves are part of equity.** The shared word “reserve” does not make them the same thing.

### 5.2 Risk-weighted and unweighted requirements

Against the advances and investment positions, **risk-weighted assets (RWA)** are exposure amounts adjusted to represent their risk under the applicable capital framework.

$$\text{Credit RWA}=\sum_i\text{Adjusted exposure}_i\times\text{Risk weight}_i$$
Adjusted exposurei = recognised exposure after applicable conversion and mitigation; risk weighti = prescribed or model-derived weighting for that exposure.

The **standardised approach** uses prescribed treatments, including relevant external ratings and exposure classes.

The **internal ratings based (IRB)** approach uses approved internal credit inputs within supervisory capital functions.

| Approach | Internal estimation | Prescribed elements |
|---|---|---|
| Standardised | Source classification and exposure information | Risk-weight and conversion treatment |
| Foundation IRB | Primarily PD | Supervisory values and functions for other required inputs |
| Advanced IRB | Broader internal estimation of credit inputs | Supervisory function and constraints |

IRB converts borrower PD into default risk conditional on severe common economic stress, applies LGD, separates expected loss and adjusts for maturity. Longer maturity gives more time for deterioration before repayment.

**Asset correlation** in this framework describes sensitivity to a common economic factor. It is distinct from an observed equity beta and from a portfolio HHI.

An **output floor** limits how far model-based RWA may fall below the prescribed standardised reference. The **use test** requires internal ratings and risk definitions to support real credit decisions, pricing and monitoring, rather than existing solely to lower reported capital.

Total RWA includes credit, market and **operational risk**, the risk of loss from failures in processes, people, systems or external events.

$$\text{Capital ratio}=\frac{\text{Eligible capital}}{\text{Total RWA}}$$
Eligible capital = the component appropriate to the ratio, such as CET1 or total capital; total RWA = risk-weighted denominator across applicable risk types.

$$\text{Leverage ratio}=\frac{\text{Tier 1 capital}}{\text{Total exposure measure}}$$
Tier 1 capital = CET1 plus AT1; total exposure measure = the applicable exposure base without risk weighting.

**Trap — low risk weights do not remove balance-sheet size.** The leverage ratio constrains exposure growth that favourable risk weights can conceal.

### 5.3 Why deterioration can hurt both sides of the ratio

An impaired advances asset can reduce capital through P&L while its risk classification changes the RWA denominator.

| Channel | Mechanism | Usual deterioration effect |
|---|---|---|
| Capital numerator | Higher impairment expense reduces retained earnings | Capital falls |
| RWA denominator | Worse credit treatment can increase risk weight or model requirement | RWA rises |
| Heavily provided defaulted exposure | Relevant exposure and provision treatment can reduce remaining RWA | RWA may fall despite a large realised loss |

**Trap — falling RWA does not establish improving financial health.** Losses may already have reduced the capital numerator.

A **capital conservation buffer** is additional capital protection associated with restrictions on distributions when it is depleted. Other buffers or surcharges depend on the bank and applicable framework.

| Basel pillar | Function | What it adds |
|---|---|---|
| Pillar 1 | Minimum capital rules | Comparable prescribed requirements |
| Pillar 2 | Supervisory review and internal assessment | Risks not adequately captured by minimum rules |
| Pillar 3 | Public disclosure | Information for market discipline |

The **Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP)** is the bank’s own assessment of capital needs, including stress and risks such as concentration.

**Stress testing** measures consequences under adverse assumptions. It must connect credit losses, earnings, RWA and capital; changing only one can miss the combined effect.

The same exposure now supplies an ECL amount, a capital requirement and regulatory classifications. They come from the same underlying record, with framework-specific transformations. They are connected outputs, not three independently maintained versions of the loan.

Capital addresses loss absorption. The deposit liability still has to be paid when due, even when the capital ratio is adequate. The next constraint is cash.

## 6. How the bank funds itself and meets payments

On the right-hand side, deposits and borrowings finance assets that often return cash later. On the left, cash and marketable investments meet withdrawals and settlement needs.

**Liquidity** is the ability to meet payments when due. **Solvency** concerns whether the bank can absorb losses and support its obligations. A solvent bank can still lack cash at the required time.

### 6.1 The funding structure

The banking-book funding lines differ by provider, maturity and behaviour. Those differences remain relevant even when two liabilities have the same accounting balance.

| Funding line | ₹ crore | Contractual feature | Main vulnerability |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Retail savings and current accounts | 500 | Repayable on demand | Withdrawals and changing aggregate stability |
| Retail term deposits | 400 | Agreed term, with applicable withdrawal rights | Early breaks and non-renewal |
| Corporate wholesale deposits | 250 | Short contractual maturities | Concentration and non-renewal |
| Financial-institution deposits | 150 | Short market-sensitive funding | Rapid withdrawal or failure to roll |
| Market and refinance borrowings | 160 | Mixed maturities | Access and replacement cost |
| Tier 2 borrowing | 40 | Longer contractual funding | Contractual repayment and capital eligibility |

**Rollover** means replacing or renewing funding when it matures. **Refinancing risk** is the risk that replacement funding is unavailable or too expensive.

**Maturity transformation** is funding longer-lived assets with shorter-lived liabilities. It creates income opportunities and exposes the bank to replacement funding and repricing risk.

**Trap — a cheap liability is not necessarily valuable stable funding.** Its withdrawal behaviour, maturity, concentration and currency matter alongside its customer rate.

### 6.2 Reserve holdings and cash access

In cash balances and government investments, the bank holds resources for both statutory requirements and liquidity management.

The **cash reserve ratio (CRR)** is the required proportion of the relevant liability base held with RBI as cash balances. The **statutory liquidity ratio (SLR)** is the required proportion maintained in eligible liquid assets.

**Net demand and time liabilities (NDTL)** is the regulatory liability base after the specified inclusions and netting. **Demand liabilities** are repayable on demand; **time liabilities** have agreed repayment terms.

| Dimension | CRR | SLR |
|---|---|---|
| Resource held | Cash balance with RBI | Eligible cash, gold or approved securities |
| Economic effect | Cash tied to the reserve requirement | Assets retained to meet the statutory requirement |
| Income characteristic | Required reserve balances do not earn ordinary lending income | Eligible securities can earn coupons |
| Reporting dependency | NDTL and reserve-maintenance records | NDTL and eligible-holding records |

**Trap — the entire RBI balance is not automatically freely usable liquidity.** Required reserves and usable surplus must be distinguished.

SLR eligibility also does not automatically establish unrestricted liquidity recognition in every other measure. Regulatory permissions, availability and encumbrance determine the usable amount.

**Encumbered** means pledged or otherwise restricted from being freely used. A security can remain an accounting asset while being unavailable for another funding purpose.

### 6.3 Repo and the Indian money market

Government securities in the investment line can support borrowing. A **repurchase agreement, or repo**, sells securities with an agreement to repurchase them; economically it commonly provides secured funding.

The **repo haircut** makes cash advanced lower than the security value recognised as collateral.

Under secured-borrowing accounting, the security remains on the asset side and the funding appears as a liability because the bank retains the relevant economic risks and rewards.

**Trap — repo does not create both free cash and an unrestricted copy of the same collateral.** The cash, borrowing and encumbrance must be recorded together.

| Market or facility | Plain-English function | Funding characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| Call, notice and term money | Interbank borrowing for agreed short periods | Unsecured funding |
| **Triparty Repo Dealing System (TREPS)** | Repo trading supported by a third-party collateral arrangement and CCIL infrastructure | Secured money-market funding |
| **Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF)** | RBI liquidity operations | Central-bank liquidity access |
| **Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)** | Standing overnight borrowing facility under its eligibility rules | Additional liquidity access |
| Treasury bills and bank or corporate paper | Short-term debt instruments | Investment or market-funding instruments |

Eligibility, available collateral, maturity and operational access decide whether a nominal resource can become cash before a deadline.

### 6.4 The thirty-day liquidity test

Across usable liquid assets and runnable liabilities, the **liquidity coverage ratio (LCR)** compares a liquidity buffer with stressed net cash outflows over 30 days.

**High quality liquid assets (HQLA)** are eligible assets that can be converted into cash under the applicable liquidity framework.

$$LCR=\frac{HQLA}{\text{Stressed 30-day net cash outflows}}$$
HQLA = eligible liquid-asset stock after required adjustments and caps; denominator = recognised stressed outflows less permitted inflows over 30 days.

Full coverage corresponds to 100%. The reporting method and applicable requirement must be associated with the correct rule version.

The numerator begins with security and cash positions, not with an accounting subtotal called “liquid investments”.

| Numerator control | What is checked | Why it changes HQLA |
|---|---|---|
| Eligibility | Asset type, issuer, rating and other required conditions | An investment may not qualify |
| Availability | Unencumbered and operationally usable amount | Restricted assets cannot be assumed available |
| Valuation | Correct market value and business date | Stale prices distort available value |
| Haircuts and composition caps | Recognised value after prescribed reductions | Face value and accounting value can overstate the buffer |

The framework distinguishes **Level 1** and **Level 2** assets. Level 2 assets receive more restrictive treatment; the standard overall Level 2 cap is 40% of HQLA. Eligible Level 2A corporate bonds carry the stated haircut treatment, including the 15% haircut in this framework.

A corporate bond’s rating alone does not establish every condition for eligibility. The whole ₹120 crore corporate investment line must not be assumed eligible merely because part of it qualifies.

**Trap — book designation alone does not establish HQLA eligibility.** Liquidity recognition also depends on the asset and its availability.

### 6.5 Building stressed outflows and permitted inflows

On the deposit and commitment records, a **run-off factor** is the prescribed share treated as leaving under the liquidity stress calculation.

$$\text{Gross stressed outflows}=\sum_i B_i\times f_i+\text{Other recognised outflows}$$
Bi = balance or commitment base for category i; fi = applicable run-off or drawdown factor; other recognised outflows = relevant contractual, collateral and other cash demands.

| Exposure category | Required classification evidence | Effect of a wrong classification |
|---|---|---|
| Stable retail deposits | Customer type and required stability or protection attributes | May receive an inappropriately low run-off |
| Less-stable retail deposits | Retail status without all stable-category conditions | Outflow treatment differs from stable retail |
| Non-financial corporate funding | Correct corporate counterparty type | Misclassification can understate wholesale outflow |
| Financial-institution funding | Correct financial-counterparty identity | Treating it as stable retail can materially inflate LCR |
| Operational deposits | Evidence of the qualifying operational relationship | Unsupported status can understate outflows |
| Undrawn commitments | Facility type, availability and commitment status | Missing headroom omits potential cash demand |
| Derivative collateral flows | Contract terms and relevant margin requirements | Ignoring margin overstates cash resilience |

An **operational deposit** is linked to qualifying operational services under the applicable definition. It cannot be identified simply because the account has transaction activity.

On the asset side, contractual receipts do not all become fully recognised inflows. Apply the applicable category factor first, then the overall cap.

$$\text{Net outflows}=O-\min(I,\;0.75\times O)$$
O = gross stressed outflows; I = eligible inflows after category-specific treatment; 0.75 = the standard overall inflow cap as a proportion of outflows.

**Trap — an inflow recognition factor and the overall inflow cap are different controls.** A factor applied to corporate receipts is not the percentage of all outflows that may be offset.

The cap prevents the bank relying entirely on anticipated receipts to meet the stress. The numerator must therefore include an actual eligible liquidity stock.

**Trap — a higher LCR does not always mean more liquidity in rupees.** When highly runnable funding leaves, both HQLA and the next period’s assumed outflows fall. The ratio can rise if its denominator falls faster.

The management view therefore includes HQLA in rupees, cash requirements, funding concentration and limit usage alongside the ratio.

### 6.6 The maturity ladder

Across assets, liabilities and commitments, a **maturity ladder** places expected receipts and payments into time intervals. The Indian **structural liquidity statement (SLS)** is the corresponding bucketed liquidity view.

A **bucket** is a defined interval. A **gap** is the difference between inflows and outflows in that interval.

$$\text{Bucket gap}_k=\text{Inflows}_k-\text{Outflows}_k$$
k = time bucket; inflowsk = recognised receipts in the bucket; outflowsk = recognised payments in the bucket.

$$\text{Cumulative gap}_k=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\text{Bucket gap}_j$$
Cumulative gapk = accumulated surplus or deficit through bucket k; j = each included earlier bucket.

**Trap — a later surplus cannot settle an earlier payment.** The cumulative near-term deficit matters even when lifetime inflows and outflows eventually balance.

| Bucket group | Intervals in the ladder | Main focus |
|---|---|---|
| Near term | Day 1; 2–7; 8–14; 15–30 days | Immediate payment and funding pressure |
| Intermediate | 31 days–2 months; 2–3; 3–6 months; 6 months–1 year | Refinancing dependence |
| Longer term | 1–3; 3–5; over 5 years | Structural maturity mismatch |

A **tolerance limit** restricts the permitted deficit, often relative to cumulative outflows. The applicable bucket definition and limit must travel together in the methodology.

A marketable government security can mature far in the future yet be realisable earlier through sale or repo. Contractual maturity and documented expected realisation therefore answer different liquidity questions.

**Trap — shifting a security into an early bucket requires a permitted, evidenced treatment.** It cannot be done simply to remove a reported deficit.

### 6.7 Contractual and behavioural maturity

For banking-book deposits and prepayable advances, actual cash behaviour can differ from legal maturity.

**Behavioural maturity** is the estimated timing of cash flows based on observed customer behaviour. A **core deposit balance** is the portion of an on-demand deposit population expected to remain stable.

| Dimension | Contractual | Behavioural |
|---|---|---|
| Basis | Legal terms and scheduled dates | Observed and modelled behaviour |
| On-demand deposits | Repayable immediately | Stable and volatile portions can behave differently |
| Prepayable loans | Contractual repayment schedule | Repayment may occur earlier |
| Evidence | Contract and account terms | History, methodology, approval and back-testing |

**Back-testing** compares assumptions or predictions with what subsequently occurred.

Core balances need relevant history, stress consideration, approval and ongoing review. Stability of aggregate balances does not mean every individual deposit remains.

**Trap — a core percentage is not proof of a particular behavioural life.** The amount regarded as stable and the length of stability both require evidence.

One governed behavioural view supports liquidity slotting, funding valuation and interest-rate risk. The regulatory LCR treatment must still follow its prescribed categories and factors; a behavioural life is not directly substituted for a run-off percentage.

### 6.8 The structural funding test

On the same liability that funds the loan, the **net stable funding ratio (NSFR)** asks whether sufficiently stable funding supports the asset structure over a one-year horizon.

**Available stable funding (ASF)** is funding recognised after weighting liabilities and capital for stability. **Required stable funding (RSF)** is the stable funding needed after weighting assets and off-balance-sheet exposures.

$$NSFR=\frac{ASF}{RSF}$$
ASF = recognised stable funding supply; RSF = recognised stable funding requirement.

$$ASF=\sum_i L_i\times a_i$$
Li = liability or capital amount in category i; ai = applicable ASF factor.

$$RSF=\sum_j A_j\times r_j+\text{Off-balance-sheet requirement}$$
Aj = asset amount in category j; rj = applicable RSF factor; off-balance-sheet requirement = recognised stable funding need from commitments and other included items.

| Side | Broad treatment direction | Critical data |
|---|---|---|
| Equity and sufficiently long funding | Greater stable-funding recognition | Capital eligibility and residual maturity |
| Qualifying retail deposits | Greater stability than short financial wholesale money | Counterparty and stability category |
| Short financial-institution funding | Low stable-funding recognition | Counterparty type and maturity |
| Cash and highly liquid assets | Lower stable-funding requirement | Asset category and availability |
| Long loans and less-liquid assets | Higher stable-funding requirement | Remaining maturity, quality and encumbrance |
| Undrawn commitments | Can create an additional requirement | Complete off-balance-sheet population |

ASF and RSF factors are regulatory weights, not the bank’s direct probability forecasts.

| Dimension | LCR | NSFR |
|---|---|---|
| Main question | Can the bank meet 30-day stressed net outflows? | Does stable funding support the structure? |
| Numerator | Eligible liquid assets | Recognised stable liabilities and capital |
| Denominator | Stressed net cash outflows | Weighted funding need of assets and commitments |
| Main management response | Change buffer and near-term cash demands | Change funding or asset composition |

**Trap — passing one ratio does not establish passing the other.** Liquidity stock and stable funding structure are different constraints.

The loan’s maturity contributes to the funding requirement. The deposit’s provider and maturity contribute to recognised funding supply. NSFR measures the structural relationship that internal funding prices will value.

### 6.9 Intraday liquidity

In cash and settlement accounts, **intraday liquidity** is the ability to meet payments within the business day.

Daily inflows can exceed daily outflows while arriving too late for time-critical obligations. The timing problem disappears in an end-of-day balance.

Key measures include peak intraday usage, available intraday resources, payment throughput and time-critical payment timing. **Throughput** describes how much payment activity has been completed through the day.

Payment-system records are therefore a distinct input. Loan and deposit closing balances cannot reconstruct every settlement deadline.

The funding question now has three clocks: payments within the day, stressed net demand over 30 days and stable support over one year. A fourth clock governs income: when rates reset.

## 7. What funding does to earnings and economic value

Across banking-book advances, investments, deposits and borrowings, money can remain available while its cost changes. **Interest rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB)** measures the effect of interest-rate changes on that structure.

**Asset and liability management (ALM)** manages the relationships between asset and funding cash flows, maturities and repricing.

### 7.1 The earnings view

On income-generating assets and interest-bearing liabilities, **net interest income (NII)** is interest income less interest expense.

$$NII=\text{Interest income}-\text{Interest expense}$$
Interest income = recognised interest on assets; interest expense = recognised funding interest cost for the same period.

A **repricing gap** compares assets and liabilities whose rates change within the selected horizon.

$$G=\text{Rate-sensitive assets}-\text{Rate-sensitive liabilities}$$
G = repricing gap; rate-sensitive assets and liabilities = amounts repricing within the defined interval.

$$\Delta NII\approx G\times\Delta r$$
ΔNII = approximate income change over the stated horizon; G = repricing gap; Δr = assumed interest-rate change as a decimal.

This is a coarse approximation. Actual timing within the horizon, balance changes and rate pass-through can alter the result.

| Position | Rates rise, other inputs fixed | Rates fall, other inputs fixed |
|---|---|---|
| Positive repricing gap | Income tends to rise faster than cost | Income tends to fall faster than cost |
| Negative repricing gap | Cost tends to rise faster than income | Cost tends to fall faster than income |

A negative gap is **liability-sensitive**: liabilities reprice more strongly or sooner within the horizon.

**Deposit beta** is the degree to which deposit rates follow changes in market rates. It modifies the assumption that every funding rate moves one-for-one.

**Trap — maturity is not the repricing date.** A long floating loan may reprice early; a fixed loan does not merely because its funding renews.

### 7.2 The lifetime-value view

Across the same banking-book cash flows, **economic value of equity (EVE)** is the present value of assets less the present value of liabilities.

$$EVE=PV(\text{Assets})-PV(\text{Liabilities})$$
PV(assets) = discounted expected asset cash flows; PV(liabilities) = discounted expected liability cash flows.

This is an economic sensitivity measure, not the reported accounting-equity balance.

$$\Delta EVE\approx-\left(D_A-D_L\times\frac{L}{A}\right)\times A\times\Delta r$$
DA and DL = asset and liability duration measures; A and L = corresponding value bases; Δr = rate shock; ΔEVE = approximate economic-value change.

Rates rising reduce the value of fixed future receipts and fixed future payments. Reduced asset value hurts EVE; reduced liability value offsets part of that loss.

| Dimension | NII sensitivity | EVE sensitivity |
|---|---|---|
| Main horizon | Selected earnings period, commonly one year | Remaining cash-flow life |
| Measures | Income and expense movement | Present-value movement |
| Main limitation | Can miss risk beyond the earnings horizon | Does not fully describe the timing of reported earnings |
| Key assumptions | Repricing, balances and deposit beta | Cash-flow timing, discounting and behavioural life |

**Trap — a stable one-year NII result does not establish low lifetime rate risk.** Conversely, acceptable EVE sensitivity does not guarantee smooth near-term earnings.

The **asset and liability committee (ALCO)** is the management forum overseeing balance-sheet structure and related limits. It needs both views, under approved assumptions.

### 7.3 The shared behavioural assumption

On core deposits and prepayable advances, changing behavioural life changes expected cash-flow timing and therefore duration. Changing deposit beta changes the earnings response to market rates.

A longer assumed stable deposit life can increase liability duration and offset more fixed-asset duration. That can improve the reported EVE result while also increasing the deposit business’s internal funding credit.

**Trap — favourable movement in two outputs is not independent evidence that the assumption is correct.** Both may come from the same optimistic model input.

A swap can offset selected rate risk, but its benchmark, reset and maturity must match the exposure being hedged closely enough. Margin and counterparty risk remain separate consequences.

Treasury now owns a measurable mismatch between asset and liability behaviour. Internal pricing assigns that mismatch to Treasury and separates it from customer-business margins.

## 8. How the spread is split internally

Between banking-book assets and their funding, **funds transfer pricing (FTP)** assigns an internal price to the use or supply of funds.

Treasury acts as the internal counterparty. Deposit businesses supply funds and receive an **FTP credit**. Lending businesses use funds and pay an **FTP charge**. These are management-accounting entries; no additional customer cash is created.

### 8.1 The three margins

For the asset and funding sides together, FTP separates customer pricing from the maturity and rate mismatch.

| Margin | Calculation | Economic responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| Lending margin | Customer asset yield less FTP charge | Customer pricing and credit economics |
| Deposit margin | FTP credit less customer funding cost | Value of gathering funding |
| Treasury margin | Internal charges less credits, with allocated Treasury income and costs | Funding, repricing and balance-sheet management |

**Trap — the lending margin is not automatically net profit.** Credit loss and other applicable costs still have to be recognised on consistent bases.

A matched FTP charge allows the lending unit’s margin to be assessed against its credit cost without also assigning it an unrelated short-funding position.

The same deposit is therefore seen two ways: **FTP prices the funding value it supplies; NSFR measures how much stable funding it contributes.** A low customer rate alone determines neither.

### 8.2 The transfer-pricing curve

For every priced asset or liability, the **FTP curve** is a published set of internal rates by tenor and currency, with an identifiable effective date.

**Marginal funding cost** is the cost of raising the next unit of funding for the relevant term. It differs from the historical average cost of existing deposits.

An **overnight indexed swap (OIS)** exchanges a fixed rate against an overnight-rate-based stream. An OIS or other swap curve can provide a market reference for the base-rate layer.

| Curve layer | What it prices | Data source |
|---|---|---|
| Base rate | Interest-rate value for the relevant tenor | Approved market reference curve |
| Bank funding spread | What the bank pays above that reference | Own issuance, wholesale funding and market spreads |
| Combined funding curve | Relevant marginal funding cost | Governed combination of the two layers |

The base-rate layer transfers interest-rate economics. The funding-spread layer transfers the bank’s funding and liquidity economics.

**Trap — the bank’s cheap existing deposit average is not the correct price for every new asset tenor.** A pooled average hides the cost of committing funding for longer.

A curve must carry its identifier, currency, publication date, tenor points, component rates and approval. **Interpolation** estimates the rate between published tenor points using a specified rule.

### 8.3 Which life is priced

For a banking-book instrument, FTP starts with the contractual schedule and applies the approved behavioural treatment where relevant.

**Weighted average life (WAL)** is the average time at which principal is repaid, weighted by principal repayments.

$$WAL=\frac{\sum_t t\times PR_t}{\sum_t PR_t}$$
PRt = principal repaid at time t; t = time from the pricing reference date in a consistent unit.

| Product | Tenor treatment | Required evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed-rate bullet loan | Contractual funding life under the matched-funding policy | Contract and origination date |
| Amortising loan | Repayment-weighted life or approved cash-flow treatment | Principal schedule |
| Revolving asset | Behavioural core and volatile portions | Balance history and model output |
| Savings or current deposits | Behavioural stable and volatile portions | Approved core-balance and life assumptions |
| Term deposit | Contractual term with relevant early-break treatment | Terms and withdrawal history |
| Prepayable loan | Prepayment-adjusted treatment | Prepayment history and methodology |

**Trap — an amortising asset is not funded at its opening balance until final maturity.** Charging it as a full-life bullet can overstate the lending unit’s funding cost.

For floating-rate instruments, separate **rate-reset tenor** from **funding life**. Repricing the base-rate component does not make a long asset an overnight liquidity commitment.

### 8.4 One behavioural view, three consequences

On the same deposit population, the behavioural evidence must be governed once and supplied to its consumers.

| Consumer | How behaviour enters | Consequence of unsupported stability |
|---|---|---|
| FTP | Assigned funding life and stable/volatile split | Deposit credit can be overstated |
| LCR and liquidity management | Relevant stability classification and permitted outflow assumptions | Stressed outflows can be understated |
| EVE | Liability cash-flow and repricing treatment | Liability duration can overstate the offset to asset risk |

**The connection is one governed view of behaviour, not one interchangeable number.** Behavioural maturity drives FTP tenor and EVE timing; the same stability evidence must support the LCR classification, while prescribed LCR factors retain their own rule basis.

The specification must reference the upstream ALM model and version. Re-entering its assumptions independently in FTP and liquidity spreadsheets creates competing versions of the same liability.

### 8.5 Origination rates and reset rates

For fixed-rate banking-book lending, **matched funding** assigns an internal funding price consistent with the asset’s agreed rate and life.

| Instrument | Curve vintage | Why |
|---|---|---|
| New business | Approved curve applicable when priced | Reflect current marginal cost |
| Existing fixed-rate asset | Retained origination assignment under policy | Avoid changing the funding price behind a fixed customer yield |
| Floating-rate asset | Approved reset treatment aligned with customer repricing | Keep rate transfer consistent with the reset structure |

A **vintage** is the date-specific version of an input or cohort. Retaining the curve vintage makes a historical charge reproducible.

**Trap — falling back to today’s curve on an existing fixed-rate loan manufactures a margin change.** A missing historical curve is an exception, not permission to substitute another date.

The source must retain origination date, next reset date, curve identifier and relevant curve date. A dated contract alone cannot reconstruct an omitted historical pricing decision.

### 8.6 The FTP charge components

Across drawn assets, commitments and customer options, the all-in funding economics contain distinct costs.

| Component | What it pays for | Main charging basis |
|---|---|---|
| Base-rate component | Interest-rate value over the assigned tenor | Drawn or funded balance |
| Term funding/liquidity premium | Cost of securing funds beyond the base curve | Relevant funding balance and life |
| Contingent liquidity charge | Resources held for possible future drawings | Undrawn commitment |
| Optionality charge | Cost of customer rights that alter cash flows | Relevant option exposure or approved spread |

The **term liquidity premium** is the incremental cost attributed to committing funding over the relevant term. In a combined curve it is already within the funding spread.

**Trap — do not add a liquidity premium twice.** A published all-in funding curve and a separate funding-spread field must have clearly different or explicitly overlapping scope.

**Contingent liquidity** is cash the bank may need if a commitment is exercised. Holding HQLA against that demand has a **cost of carry**: funding cost less the income earned on the buffer assets.

$$\text{Annual contingent cost}=U\times d\times c$$
U = undrawn commitment; d = assigned stressed drawdown fraction; c = annual cost of carrying the associated liquidity buffer.

$$\text{Undrawn charge rate}=d\times c$$
d = drawdown fraction; c = buffer cost of carry; the resulting rate is charged against the undrawn base under policy.

This identifies a funding-cost component that a commitment fee may recover. It does not make every commercial commitment fee solely a liquidity charge.

The commitment now has three linked outputs: converted credit exposure, stressed liquidity demand and an internal cost for supplying that liquidity. All start from the same undrawn amount; their factors have different purposes.

**Optionality** is the value of a contractual right to alter payment timing or terms. Borrower prepayment and early deposit withdrawal can leave Treasury’s funding position mismatched.

An approved approach can price an option explicitly and/or reflect behavioural cash-flow changes. The policy must identify what each adjustment captures.

**Trap — shortening average life is not automatically a complete price for an option.** Avoid both ignoring the option and charging twice for the same effect.

### 8.7 Computing the amount and proving the allocation

On each priced balance, a **day-count convention** determines the fraction of a year represented by the accrual period.

$$\text{FTP amount}=B\times r\times\alpha$$
B = relevant balance; r = annual FTP rate as a decimal; α = year fraction under the specified day-count convention.

Daily balances can matter for revolving products. A month-end balance does not describe funding used throughout the month.

For a blended assignment:

$$\bar r=\frac{\sum_i B_i r_i}{\sum_i B_i}$$
r̄ = balance-weighted FTP rate; Bi = balance in slice i; ri = assigned rate for that slice.

**Trap — a rate applied to undrawn commitments is not applied to the drawn balance as well merely because the output is called “all-in”.** Preserve each component’s charging base.

All internal charges and credits must cancel on consolidation.

$$\sum_u\text{Unit NII contribution}_u=\text{Bank NII}$$
u = each included business unit; unit NII contribution = external interest plus allocated internal interest on the agreed perimeter; bank NII = external net interest income on that same perimeter.

A residual points to missing or duplicated balances, unmatched internal entries, inconsistent accruals or differing reporting scope.

Treasury’s result also depends on where the funding benefit of equity and other non-interest-bearing balances is allocated. The **equity credit rate** is the policy rate used for that allocation.

**Trap — Treasury margin is not pure maturity-transformation profit.** Investment income, funding allocations, hedges and equity-credit policy can also affect it.

### 8.8 Data and governance for FTP

For each asset and liability, the FTP record must explain the balance, rate, life and organisational destination of the charge.

| Data group | Required content | Number protected |
|---|---|---|
| Instrument | Product, currency, rate type, repayment shape and maturity | Correct method and curve |
| Behaviour | Life, stable/volatile split, prepayment and early-break assumptions | Correct assigned tenor |
| Balance | Drawn amount, relevant accruals and undrawn availability | Correct charging base |
| Curve | Identifier, date, tenor points, base rate and spread | Correct rate and vintage |
| Conventions | Origination, reset, reporting dates and day-count basis | Correct accrual |
| Organisation | Business unit, cost centre and booking entity | Correct margin owner |

A **cost centre** is the organisational code receiving an allocated cost or income. A **booking entity** is the legal entity in which the transaction is recorded.

Missing behavioural life must not silently become overnight. Missing commitment data must not silently mean no commitment. Missing historical rates must not be replaced by current rates.

FTP policy needs published methodology, independent ownership from the units being priced and controlled changes. Negotiating a rate simply to achieve a business unit’s target destroys the measurement.

The spread is now allocated, but every allocation depends on a product field, a model input or a dated curve. The next task is to establish where each comes from and how its effect can be proved.

## 9. Where the numbers come from

Across both sides of the balance sheet and the off-balance-sheet commitments, risk measurement begins with identified records. A calculation cannot repair a missing borrower, an omitted deposit or an incorrect reporting date.

### 9.1 One exposure record, several measurement bases

For advances, investments and derivatives, a **data dictionary** gives each field an agreed meaning, unit, scope and source.

**Granularity** is the level of detail retained. The **grain** of a table states exactly what one row represents.

| Record type | Grain | Why it must remain distinct |
|---|---|---|
| Exposure position | Identified facility or instrument at a date | Starting population for measurement |
| Contractual cash flow | Payment or receipt on a date | Timing for valuation, funding and repricing |
| Collateral allocation | Protection linked to a specified exposure | Prevents unsupported or duplicated recognition |
| Calculated result | Exposure, date, method and run | Preserves the basis of ECL, EAD or RWA |

One exposure can have many cash flows and collateral links. Joining those records without respecting their grain can multiply balances.

**Trap — “one source of truth” does not mean one universal exposure amount.** Contractual principal, GCA, derivative market value, converted EAD and RWA answer different questions.

A **key** is a field or combination of fields identifying or linking records. Obligor, facility, instrument, entity and reporting-date keys make cross-product aggregation possible.

A **reference-data set** contains controlled classifications or mappings used across records. Product hierarchy, sector, counterparty type and business-unit codes belong here.

A **golden source** is the designated authoritative source for a data element. Authority belongs to the particular element, not automatically to every field in a favoured system.

### 9.2 Risk-data governance

For every balance-sheet line, the **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS)** principles known as **BCBS 239** address risk-data aggregation and risk reporting.

**Data aggregation** combines granular records into totals and views. It must remain accurate when the question crosses products, entities and geographies.

| Area | Requirement | Operational evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Governance and infrastructure | Accountable ownership and suitable architecture | Approved ownership, policies and design |
| Aggregation | Accurate, complete, timely and adaptable data | Reconciliations, controls and granular records |
| Risk reporting | Clear, comprehensive and decision-relevant output | Defined content, recipients and production timing |
| Supervisory review | Demonstrable compliance and remediation | Evidence, tracked gaps and completed actions |

**Accuracy** means the values are correct. **Completeness** means the required population and attributes are present. **Timeliness** means the information arrives early enough for its purpose. **Adaptability** means new questions can be answered without rebuilding the reporting estate.

**Trap — a reliable fixed report does not prove adaptability.** Pre-aggregated totals can produce an existing return perfectly while making a new cross-product exposure question impossible.

A named **data owner** is accountable for a critical element’s definition and quality. A support team can fix a feed; it cannot decide what the field ought to mean.

### 9.3 Who owns the number

For assets, funding and their reported measures, responsibility separates position ownership, methodology, accounting and production.

| Owner | Accountability | Evidence supplied |
|---|---|---|
| Treasury and ALM | Funding position, relevant behavioural assumptions and internal funding prices | Approved assumptions, curves and liquidity views |
| Risk | Risk methods, limits and independent challenge | Definitions, model approvals and limit framework |
| Finance | General ledger and accounting basis | Balances, journals and reconciliation baseline |
| Regulatory Reporting | Return interpretation, mapping and submission | Reporting methodology, review and attestation |
| **Business as usual (BAU)** team | Recurring production and operational controls | Run records, exceptions and close evidence |
| Technology | Data pipeline, application and release | Implemented transformations and operational evidence |
| Vendor | Supplied engine and documented product logic | Capability, release content and defect fixes |

The **general ledger (GL)** is Finance’s accounting record of balances and transactions. A **journal** is an accounting entry recorded in it.

**Trap — the vendor owns a product, not the bank’s regulatory interpretation.** Configuration and input quality remain the bank’s responsibilities.

When two owners defend different numbers, compare their population, date, valuation and rule basis before deciding which calculation is defective.

### 9.4 The three kinds of wrong

On a reported deposit, loan or security, **data quality (DQ)** controls protect the business meaning of the number. Defects have different causes and owners.

| Defect | Symptom | Accountable fix | Corrective action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Source | Original value is wrong or absent | Source business owner | Correct the record and its capture control |
| Transformation | Source is right; target differs incorrectly | Technology | Correct code against the approved rule |
| Definition | Competing interpretations each appear defensible | Business methodology owner | Resolve the meaning, amend specification and retest |

**Trap — a definition defect is not resolved by proving the code matches an ambiguous specification.** The implemented rule can be technically faithful and economically wrong.

A **DQ rule** is an executable check of an agreed requirement. It needs a threshold, severity, owner and exception action.

| DQ dimension | What is tested | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Completeness | Population and required fields present | Source counts and amount totals |
| Validity | Values belong to allowed domains | Category and range checks |
| Consistency | Related attributes agree | Cross-field and relationship checks |
| Accuracy | Values agree with independent evidence | Ledger or authoritative-source reconciliation |
| Timeliness | Business dates and updates meet the cut-off | Date and feed-completion checks |
| Uniqueness | Records are not duplicated at the required grain | Key checks |

### 9.5 One field, one reported consequence

Within each asset or funding record, the DQ rule exists to protect a particular output. The reason for the rule belongs beside the check.

| Critical field | Defect | Number affected | Protective rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deposit counterparty type | Financial funding treated as stable retail | Outflows understated; LCR overstated | Require supported classification; reject an underivable category |
| Original credit-risk measure | Origination history missing | SICR and allowance horizon unreliable | Retain the required origination basis |
| DPD and default evidence | Null becomes zero or trigger omitted | Classification and allowance understated | Validate payment history and required trigger evidence |
| Recovery amount and date | Stale favourable valuation or missing delay | LGD and allowance understated | Require current approved input and recovery timing |
| EIR | Contractual coupon substituted | Carrying amount and discounted loss distorted | Source the effective yield and its basis |
| Undrawn amount | Missing becomes zero | EAD, cash demand and FTP cost understated | Prove limit, utilisation and availability completeness |
| Book designation | Instrument routed incorrectly | Wrong risk calculation or return | Govern designation and authorised changes |
| Security eligibility and encumbrance | Unavailable asset counted as liquid | HQLA overstated | Validate eligibility and usable amount |
| Maturity and next reset | Dates interchanged | NSFR, liquidity or rate-risk buckets distorted | Hold distinct dated fields |
| Behavioural life | Unsupported value or silent overnight default | FTP allocation and EVE distorted | Reference approved model output and version |
| Curve date | Current curve replaces origination vintage | Existing fixed-loan margin distorted | Require the appropriate historical curve |
| Currency and conversion basis | Missing or inconsistent conversion | INR exposure and reconciliation distorted | Use controlled currency and dated conversion |
| Entity and obligor keys | Omitted or mismatched records | Incomplete group exposure or duplicated balances | Referential and population controls |

**Trap — rejecting a record must not erase it from the bank’s exposure.** Hold the exception visibly, retain its amount and resolve or formally account for its treatment before submission.

A secured exposure missing a required collateral link is different from an unsecured exposure. Requiring collateral for every Stage 3 record would reject legitimate unsecured lending.

Similarly, a Stage 1 record beyond the SICR overdue backstop requires investigation of any permitted rebuttal; it is not automatically evidence that no approved explanation can exist.

### 9.6 Exceptions and control totals

At every hand-off carrying balance-sheet data, a **control total** is a source-generated count or amount used to establish population integrity.

An **exception queue** holds records failing an agreed rule, with enough information to diagnose and resolve the failure.

An exception needs the record key, rule, affected amount, reporting date, cause, owner and due date. Resolution may correct source data, code or definition. A permitted temporary override needs a named approver and reason.

**Trap — a technically successful batch can contain economically incomplete output.** Completion status and population completeness are different controls.

Source totals must reconcile to accepted records, held exceptions and documented exclusions without double counting. Currency, amount basis and grain must match before totals are compared.

Track aged exceptions and recurring manual adjustments. A low count of long-unresolved material issues can be more serious than many fresh, immaterial exceptions.

The immediate correction gets the cycle completed. A source fix and preventive capture control stop recurrence. All three can be necessary.

## 10. How the numbers are proved and submitted

The same advances, investments and liabilities now become several regulatory and management outputs. A **regulatory return** is a defined submission to the supervisor; a management report supports internal decisions.

### 10.1 The reporting estate

The **reporting estate** is the complete inventory of outputs, their populations, owners, dependencies and deadlines.

| Output | Balance-sheet coverage | Core content | Main proving point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capital adequacy | Assets, commitments and capital | Capital components, RWA and ratios | Exposure-to-RWA bridge and capital reconciliation |
| Asset classification and provisioning | Advances | Classification, NPAs and provisions | Account population and provision basis |
| CRILC | Relevant borrower relationships | Exposure and stress information | Obligor aggregation and consistent classification |
| Large exposures | Relevant claims and commitments | Single and connected-group exposure | Group completeness and eligible-capital denominator |
| LCR | Liquid assets and stressed funding demand | HQLA, outflows, inflows and ratio | Eligibility, category factors and caps |
| NSFR | Assets, liabilities and commitments | ASF, RSF and ratio | Category, maturity and funding weights |
| SLS | Asset and liability cash flows | Bucket and cumulative gaps | Cash-flow completeness and slotting |
| Interest-rate sensitivity | Banking-book assets and funding | Repricing and sensitivity results | Reset dates and behavioural assumptions |
| Form A and reserve-maintenance reporting | NDTL, RBI balances and eligible holdings | Liability-base and required-reserve evidence | Correct template scope and maintenance records |
| Intraday liquidity | Payment and settlement accounts | Usage, available resources and timing | Payment-system completeness |
| Pillar 3 disclosure | Risk and capital position | Public disclosure | Consistency with approved underlying figures |
| Internal liquidity pack | Funding position and liquid resources | Buffer, funding need, concentration and limits | Availability before decisions are made |

**Form A** belongs to the Indian reserve-reporting process. CRR and SLR maintenance evidence must each be mapped to its applicable return; a broad reserve-reporting label must not replace exact template scope.

The calendar distinguishes reporting period, data cut-off, production frequency and filing deadline. Daily internal liquidity, fortnightly reserve or structural-liquidity cycles, monthly liquidity or sensitivity reporting and quarterly close processes require different dependencies.

**Trap — do not infer a filing schedule from a ratio’s measurement horizon.** Thirty-day stress does not mean “report once every thirty days”.

Each return’s actual schedule, entity scope and governing instruction must be effective-dated. A reporting inventory is a controlled operational record.

Related overseas terminology can enter a group reporting environment, without replacing the Indian mapping.

| Term | Plain-English scope | Relationship |
|---|---|---|
| **Additional Liquidity Monitoring Metrics (ALMM)** | Additional supervisory liquidity measures | Includes maturity and funding information |
| **Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA) 110** | Cash-flow mismatch reporting in the relevant overseas regime | Related to liquidity timing; not an Indian return |
| **Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process (ILAAP)** | Internal assessment of liquidity adequacy | Liquidity-specific adequacy assessment |
| **Common Reporting (COREP)** | Prudential reporting framework | Includes capital-related reporting |
| **Financial Reporting (FINREP)** | Financial reporting framework | Accounting-oriented reporting |

These are related outputs, not one-to-one substitutes. Their scope and rules require their own approved mappings.

### 10.2 The same exposure in several returns

For a banking-book loan, the accounting amount, default status and funding cash flows arise from the same identified exposure.

| Destination | Derived view | Must remain linked to |
|---|---|---|
| Accounting and provision output | GCA, allowance, net value and expense | Effective yield, stage and allowance walk |
| Capital return | Recognised exposure, risk weight and RWA | Same facility, date, protection and classification |
| CRILC | Borrower-level exposure and stress | Shared obligor and facility population |
| Large-exposure return | Aggregated claim against limit | Connected-counterparty structure |
| Liquidity statement | Expected receipts in time buckets | Repayment and impairment-adjusted cash-flow treatment |

**Trap — consistency does not mean equal amounts.** The same facility can have different principal, carrying amount, EAD and RWA. The required proof is a controlled bridge between them.

The government-security position also has several views: accounting value, statutory eligibility, usable HQLA, RSF treatment, expected cash realisation and rate sensitivity. Each view must originate from the same security position and date.

### 10.3 Reconciliation and movement analysis

Across every balance-sheet line, **reconciliation** explains differences between views of the same position. **Variance analysis** explains changes between periods.

| Control | Comparison | Required explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Population reconciliation | Source versus target at the same date | Missing, extra, held or excluded records |
| Accounting reconciliation | Risk data versus GL | Scope, valuation, accrual and accounting adjustments |
| Cross-return reconciliation | Related outputs for the same period | Different definitions and aggregation rules |
| Variance analysis | Current versus previous period | Business, methodology, data or correction drivers |

**Trap — RWA does not equal the ledger’s advances balance.** Reconcile the underlying exposure to the ledger, then explain conversion, mitigation and risk weighting.

Differences can arise from net versus gross presentation, accrued interest, currency conversion, off-balance-sheet inclusion, differing entity scope or cut-off. Quantifying the difference is only the first step.

A movement explanation identifies new lending, repayments, migrations, defaults, parameter changes, market movements, overlays and approved corrections where relevant. System or methodology changes can also cause genuine movements; they must be labelled rather than disguised as business activity.

**Trap — agreeing closing totals can conceal offsetting errors.** Analyse relevant categories and the underlying contributors.

### 10.4 Lineage and the first broken hand-off

For any submitted amount, **data lineage** is the documented path back through its calculations and transformations to the source fields.

The path must identify source record, business date, extraction run, transformations, reference data, model or rule version, result and return mapping.

A **snapshot** is a retained view of data for a particular date and run. A **cut-off** defines what information belongs in a reporting cycle.

A valuation entered after extraction can leave the frozen reporting snapshot stale even when a later live calculation is correct. The defect is timing, not necessarily arithmetic.

**Trap — a valuation-age check alone cannot detect every missed same-day update.** Update completeness and extract sequencing must also be controlled.

A **downstream extract** is a file or data set leaving one platform layer for another.

| Validation layer | Evidence | Failure isolated |
|---|---|---|
| Completeness | Counts and amount totals, including exceptions | Lost or duplicated population |
| Reconciliation | Independent accounting or source comparison | Different position or basis |
| Rule validation | Independent recomputation from the specification | Incorrect transformation or calculation |
| Regression comparison | Previously established behaviour remains correct | Unintended change |

Find the first boundary at which the controlled population or value changes incorrectly. A later calculation can be internally correct while inheriting an upstream error.

The remediation must fix the class of failure: scheduling, capture rules, mapping or methodology. Re-running without correcting the dependency leaves the same defect available for the next cycle.

### 10.5 The controlled production cycle

For the whole balance sheet, production freezes a defined position, computes the required views and produces evidence before submission.

| Stage | Required result | Gate |
|---|---|---|
| Cut-off and input acceptance | Identified, complete reporting snapshot | Required feeds and exceptions accounted for |
| Transformation and calculation | Reproducible exposure-level results | Approved rules, parameters and dates |
| Reconciliation | Explained differences to accounting and source | Material breaks resolved or formally treated |
| Movement analysis | Explained changes from the previous period | Drivers supported by records |
| Review and challenge | Independent assessment of accuracy and completeness | Reviewer evidence |
| Sign-off and attestation | Accountable approval of the specific output | Named authorised approvers |
| Submission | Filed output and retained evidence | Deadline and submission status |
| Post-submission control | Corrections and recurring defects tracked | Defined restatement route |

**Four-eyes review** means someone other than the preparer reviews the output. **Attestation** is a formal statement by an accountable person about its accuracy and completeness.

An **audit trail** records who changed or approved what, when and why. A **restatement** is a correction to a previously reported period.

Manual adjustments require the affected records or return cells, reason, amount, preparer, approver and permanent-remediation reference. The submitted figure must be reproducible including those adjustments.

**Trap — a recurring adjustment is evidence about the system, not merely about the current return.** It can indicate that the production process depends on an unimplemented rule or unresolved source defect.

The daily internal liquidity pack has its own decision deadline. It cannot be treated as an incidental export from a later regulatory close.

The reporting chain is proved only when the bank can move in both directions: from source record to submitted amount, and from a challenged amount back to its source and approvals.

## 11. How the reporting machine is built and changed

The machine must preserve the advances, investment, funding and commitment records through every calculation. Its design is the implementation of the bank’s definitions, not a substitute for them.

### 11.1 The documents that make the build testable

For each affected balance-sheet line and return, the documentation narrows from purpose to calculation to executable behaviour.

| Artefact | Required content | Approval |
|---|---|---|
| Concept paper | Current problem, target state, scope, options, benefits and major dependencies | Sponsor and affected business owners |
| Methodology paper | Regulatory basis, calculation, assumptions, boundaries and limitations | Methodology owner and Regulatory Reporting |
| **Business requirements document (BRD)** | Required behaviour, data, controls, outputs, timing and acceptance criteria | Impacted business stakeholders |
| Mapping specification | Source fields, target fields, transformations and exceptions | Named owner of each business rule |
| Feed specification | Population, format, timing, history and control totals | Source owner and Technology |
| Test strategy and pack | Coverage, expected results, evidence and release gates | Business and test owners |

**Functional requirements** state what the system must do. **Non-functional requirements** state conditions such as volume, timing, retention, availability and auditability.

**Acceptance criteria** define observable conditions for passing a requirement. “Calculate correctly” is insufficient without the governing rule, population and expected behaviour.

A classification requirement must define allowed categories, derivation inputs, treatment when classification fails, population reconciliation and accountable ownership.

A **traceability matrix** links each requirement to its methodology, mapping, implemented component, test and evidence. Unlinked requirements can remain unbuilt; unlinked code can change a number without an approved requirement.

**Trap — a vendor release note is not the bank’s methodology paper.** Product behaviour still needs to be compared with the bank’s approved interpretation.

### 11.2 Selecting a new source

For a required loan, deposit or security field, source analysis begins with the reported number it serves.

| Step | Decision | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Confirm need | Which calculation and output depend on it? | Requirement and field definition |
| Find candidates | Which systems hold a potentially relevant value? | Candidate inventory |
| Assess authority | Which source is maintained for the relevant purpose? | Ownership and operational use |
| Profile | What values and history actually exist? | Nulls, counts, distributions and dates |
| Resolve gaps | What must be captured, corrected or derived? | Owned remediation plan |
| Specify delivery | How does usable data arrive on time? | Feed and mapping specifications |

**Data profiling** measures the actual content and quality of a source before relying on it.

Source assessment must cover update frequency, lag, historical retention, extract window and changes to historical records. A populated field maintained only at origination may be unsuitable for current collateral valuation.

**Trap — an available column is not necessarily an available source.** Authority, quality, history and timing decide whether it can support the return.

### 11.3 The pipeline and its physical layers

Across all balance-sheet feeds, **extract, transform, load (ETL)** moves source data into usable reporting structures.

**Extract** copies the required source population. **Transform** applies standardisation, joins, enrichment and business rules. **Load** writes the results into the target structures.

| Layer | What it holds | Design obligation |
|---|---|---|
| Source systems | Transactions, contracts, customers, collateral and accounting | Known population and cut-off |
| Staging | Raw extracts as received | Preserve original bytes or records and run identity |
| Risk data warehouse | Conformed granular records and controlled links | Consistent definitions and reproducible history |
| Calculation engines | Exposure-level risk, liquidity and pricing results | Approved methods and versioned inputs |
| Reporting layer | Aggregations, template cells and submission outputs | Traceable mapping and review evidence |

**Staging** is the raw landing area. **Immutable** means a retained extract cannot silently be overwritten. A **data warehouse** is an organised store supporting combined analysis and reporting.

**Trap — overwriting last period’s source snapshot destroys the evidence needed to explain a movement.** Retaining only the latest balance is insufficient for restatement and parallel run.

| Extract choice | Full | Incremental |
|---|---|---|
| Population sent | Entire agreed population | Changes since a defined point |
| Main advantage | Direct population comparison | Lower transfer volume |
| Main risk | Processing may exceed the available window | Missed updates or deletions corrupt reconstructed state |
| Required proof | Complete source totals | Complete change capture and periodic population proof |

Incremental processing must capture closures and deletions as well as additions and updates. Otherwise the direction of the error depends on what was missed; it is not always an understatement.

A **batch window** is the time available for scheduled processing. Define start, dependencies, latest completion and downstream engine start in a stated time zone.

Late-arriving data, backdated transactions and prior-period corrections need explicit treatment. “Use the latest available data” does not define a reproducible reporting snapshot.

### 11.4 Database structure and investigation

In the warehouse and result layers, **Oracle** is a relational database platform. A **relational database** stores data in tables linked by keys.

| Database concept | Plain meaning | Reporting consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Schema | Named collection of database objects | Identifies the layer and ownership |
| View | Saved query presented as a table | Logic may sit in the view definition |
| Partition | Physical subdivision of a table | Can support efficient date-based processing |
| Stored procedure | Executable logic inside the database | Business transformations can live outside the vendor engine |
| Referential integrity | Valid links between related records | Prevents orphaned or inconsistent relationships |

**Trap — partitioning by date does not by itself preserve history.** Retained snapshots, versioned rules and controlled restatements make a prior result reproducible.

**Structured Query Language (SQL)** retrieves, joins and aggregates relational data. Its investigative purpose is to isolate the records explaining a control failure or movement.

| Investigation | Query objective | Evidence produced |
|---|---|---|
| Reconciliation | Aggregate on the same date, entity and amount basis | Difference by relevant category |
| Movement | Compare facilities across periods | Balance, rating and classification changes |
| Lineage | Follow one key through layers | First point of divergence |
| DQ | Find nulls, duplicates and invalid relationships | Affected population and amount |
| Control verification | Find records violating the approved rule, including exception conditions | Breaches or a justified empty result |
| Acceptance testing | Independently implement the specified calculation | Expected-versus-actual difference |

**Set-based processing** applies logic to a collection of records rather than manually processing accounts one at a time.

**Trap — a join that duplicates a facility can create an apparently valid higher total.** Check row counts and grain before and after joins, particularly for collateral and cash-flow relationships.

### 11.5 The mapping contract

For every critical source-to-target movement, the mapping specification must state enough to reproduce the target field.

| Specification element | Required precision |
|---|---|
| Source system and field | Exact authoritative origin |
| Target field and grain | Where the result lands and what one row means |
| Transformation | Complete rule, precedence, units and dates |
| Null handling | Reject, derive or approved default; with justification |
| Dependencies | Reference data, model or curve version used |
| Owner | Named person accountable for meaning |
| Regulatory or policy reference | Basis and effective version |
| Control | How completeness and correctness are checked |

**Null** means unknown or absent. Zero is a known value. Converting one into the other can change the reported economics without causing a technical error.

Mappings must keep raw evidence and derived decisions separate: actual facility arrears versus obligor classification; contractual maturity versus behavioural life; original valuation versus adjusted recognised collateral.

A model output consumed by several engines should be referenced as a dependency, not copied into several specifications as a constant.

### 11.6 Dependencies, release planning and handover

A change to one balance-sheet feed can affect multiple outputs. A **dependency map** records the upstream items each calculation needs and the downstream items each change can affect.

New products alter classification needs. New entities alter population coverage. Behavioural recalibration affects liquidity, rate risk and FTP. A chart-of-accounts change affects ledger reconciliation. A vendor release can alter calculations.

The **chart of accounts** is Finance’s structured list of ledger accounts.

| Delivery record | Meaning | Required discipline |
|---|---|---|
| Risk | Something adverse might happen | Owner, impact, mitigation and review date |
| Assumption | Something is treated as true without proof | Validation date and escalation route |
| Issue | A problem has occurred | Action owner and resolution date |
| Dependency | Delivery needs another item or party | Named owners on both sides and due date |

Together these form a **risks, assumptions, issues and dependencies (RAID)** log.

**Trap — an unvalidated source-quality assumption is unfinished analysis.** It can become a reporting defect as soon as production depends on it.

**Change** implements new capability; **run** produces the recurring outputs. Reporting deadlines and release plans compete for the same people and processing windows.

Protect reporting cut-offs and critical production periods with planned deployment restrictions. Schedule work using actual close demands, rather than assuming business reviewers remain fully available during submission.

Across centres, use one approved methodology, documented local variations, shared reference-data ownership and explicit time zones.

Prioritisation follows regulatory deadline, current reporting risk, dependency impact, benefit and then effort. An easy change is not necessarily the most valuable change.

## 12. How the machine and its models are proved

For each asset, liability and commitment affected by a change, testing must establish both correct output and controlled failure. A plausible number is not evidence of correctness.

### 12.1 Expected results before execution

A **test case** is a defined input and expected behaviour tied to a requirement. Its expected result must be established before the system’s actual result is seen.

| Test-pack element | Required content |
|---|---|
| Requirement link | Approved requirement and methodology reference |
| Input | Identified records, attributes and dates |
| Expected result | Independently determined category, amount and downstream effect |
| Actual result | Captured output from the identified run |
| Evidence | Comparison, pass/fail and defect reference |

Testing a classification alone does not prove the ratio. The evidence should connect source attribute, assigned category, factor, amount, aggregation and final reported result where those links are affected.

**Trap — deriving the expected result from the engine output only proves that the output was copied.**

### 12.2 Coverage and test stages

Across the whole reporting population, **coverage** means the requirements, rule branches and operational conditions actually exercised by testing.

| Coverage | Required proof | Failure it catches |
|---|---|---|
| Requirement | Each requirement has evidence | Omitted functionality |
| Rule branch | Every material branch is exercised | Untested category or treatment |
| Boundary | Threshold and date edges behave correctly | Incorrect inclusions and cut-offs |
| Negative | Invalid or missing input follows the approved path | Silent favourable defaults |
| Volume | Full population completes within the batch window | Correct but late output |
| End to end | Records reach the correct submitted output | Broken connections between components |

| Test stage | Purpose | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Unit | One component follows its specification | Component result |
| Integration | Components exchange and interpret data correctly | Interface and pipeline result |
| **User acceptance testing (UAT)** | Business confirms intended behaviour | Approved expected-versus-actual evidence |
| Regression | Existing correct behaviour remains correct | Comparison against established checks |
| Parallel run | Old and new outputs are compared on a common basis | Explained line-by-line differences |

**Trap — the number of test cases does not establish coverage.** Missing reject-path testing can leave the most consequential DQ control unproved.

### 12.3 Defects and cutover

On a changed return, **defect severity** expresses the business consequence, not the technical difficulty of fixing it.

| Severity | Consequence | Release treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Return unavailable or materially wrong | Resolve before cutover |
| High | Material behaviour wrong for an affected population | Resolve or use explicitly approved treatment |
| Medium | Limited, immaterial defect | Controlled deferred fix where permitted |
| Low | Cosmetic issue | Managed backlog |

A **backlog** is the prioritised list of work not yet completed.

Triage the defect as source, transformation or definition before assignment. Retest against the original expected result with independent evidence; closure must not rely only on the fixer’s assertion.

A **parallel run** compares old and new systems for the same reporting period and population. Every material difference needs a cause, intended treatment and approval.

**Trap — agreement with the old system is not proof of correctness.** An approved correction can deliberately change the result. An unexplained difference remains a defect until resolved.

**Cutover** is the switch to the new production process. **Rollback** is the controlled return to the previous process if the switch fails.

The gate requires accepted UAT, resolved material defects, an approved difference log, a tested rollback route and a named go/no-go decision. The first production cycle needs heightened review.

Handover includes operating instructions, ownership, known limitations, exception handling and retained evidence. Completion is the first successfully controlled production cycle, not merely deployment.

### 12.4 The models supplying the fields

Under advances, deposits and commitments, a **model** turns input data and assumptions into an estimate. PD, LGD, CCF, deposit behaviour and forward-looking economic assumptions all require this machinery.

A **scorecard** combines borrower characteristics into a score used for ranking or rating. Inputs can include indebtedness, ability to meet interest, business size, sector conditions and payment behaviour.

**Logistic regression** maps a weighted combination of inputs into a probability between zero and one.

$$PD=\frac{1}{1+\exp\left[-\left(\beta_0+\sum_i\beta_i x_i\right)\right]}$$
PD = predicted default probability; β0 = intercept; βi = fitted weight on driver xi; exp = exponential function; i = each input driver.

The model’s score direction must be documented. A “higher is safer” score has the opposite risk direction from a “higher is riskier” score.

Interpretability, stability and reproducibility help determine technique choice. Economically sensible directional behaviour must be checked; the technique’s name does not guarantee it.

**Calibration** can adjust probability levels without necessarily changing borrower ranking. LGD and CCF models require methods appropriate to their target quantities and boundary behaviour.

### 12.5 Ranking, levels and population fit

For the PDs feeding allowance and capital, three validation questions are independent.

| Question | Property | Measurement |
|---|---|---|
| Are riskier borrowers ranked worse? | Discrimination | Separation of defaulters and non-defaulters |
| Are probability levels right? | Calibration | Predicted versus observed default rates |
| Does the current population still fit the evidence? | Stability | Change in input or score distributions |

The **receiver operating characteristic (ROC)** curve compares the share of defaulters identified with the share of non-defaulters flagged across cut-offs.

**Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC)** measures ranking separation. It can be read as the probability that a randomly selected defaulter receives a worse risk ranking than a randomly selected non-defaulter.

**Gini** rescales AUROC.

$$Gini=2\times AUROC-1$$
AUROC = area under the ROC curve; Gini = corresponding rescaled discrimination measure.

The **Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS)** statistic measures the greatest separation between the cumulative score distributions of defaulters and non-defaulters.

**Trap — strong discrimination does not establish correct PD levels.** A model can rank borrowers well while understating every allowance and capital input.

Calibration compares predicted and observed defaults by grade and relevant segment, with uncertainty considered. An **obligor-year** is one borrower observed over one annual period.

Portfolio averages can conceal grades that are wrong in opposite directions. PIT and TTC comparisons also need compatible horizons and observation conditions.

The **population stability index (PSI)** measures shifts in distribution between a reference population and the current one.

$$PSI=\sum_i(A_i-E_i)\times\ln\left(\frac{A_i}{E_i}\right)$$
Ai = current population share in band i; Ei = reference share in that band; ln = natural logarithm; bands and treatment of zero shares must be specified.

**Trap — PSI is not a loss estimate or an overlay amount.** Drift prompts investigation of cause and model suitability; it does not automatically quantify the financial correction.

### 12.6 Model governance and implementation proof

Across the models supplying both sides of the balance sheet, accountability must separate development from independent challenge.

| Line of defence | Responsibility | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| First | Develop, use, document and monitor | Owned model and performance records |
| Second | Independently validate and challenge | Approval, conditions or rejection |
| Third | Audit the governance and its operation | Assurance over the first two lines |

A **model inventory** lists every model, purpose, owner, version, materiality and validation status. **Tiering** scales scrutiny to importance.

Validation covers conceptual soundness, source data, implementation, outcomes, benchmarks and limitations.

**Look-ahead information** is data that would not have been available when a prediction was made. Including it can make development performance look better than production performance.

**Trap — a correct methodology can be implemented incorrectly.** Independent recomputation tests production code, including data transformations and parameter versions.

A **challenger model** is an alternative used to benchmark the production model. More complex machine-learning methods can expose performance gaps, but deployment still requires suitable explainability, governance and evidence for the intended use.

A model limitation must remain attached to its use. Monitoring thresholds require actions and owners; independent validation must be able to withhold approval.

The complete chain is now testable: product terms create a dated exposure; approved methods value and measure it; funding assumptions price and support it; controlled records preserve it; reconciliations explain it; and accountable sign-off authorises the return.

## 14. One-page compression

Across the whole balance sheet, the journey from one rupee of exposure to a submitted return reduces to these connections.

1. **Assets are funded by liabilities and equity; losses reduce equity, while payments require cash.**
2. **Book designation and accounting classification route the instrument to its measurement basis, risk framework and returns.**
3. **Principal, GCA, market value, EAD and RWA are different measures of the same identified position.**
4. **PD measures default likelihood; LGD measures loss severity; EAD measures the amount exposed.**
5. **Recovery value falls when costs or collection delays rise; collateral value alone is not recovery.**
6. **Undrawn commitments create credit exposure, stressed cash demand and a cost of supplying liquidity.**
7. **Marginal PD weights period losses; conditional PD first needs survival weighting.**
8. **Correlation changes aggregate tail risk; summing individual expected losses does not require independence.**
9. **EIR carries the original lending yield; current market yield reprices a bond.**
10. **Yield or credit spread up means conventional bond value down; DV01 expresses rate sensitivity in money.**
11. **Rating and PD deterioration inform SICR; stage changes alter the allowance horizon and then the P&L charge.**
12. **Stage 1 restricts the default window; it does not restrict resulting losses to twelve months.**
13. **Stage 3 uses expected collections; interest moves to the net carrying amount.**
14. **The allowance is a stock; impairment expense is a flow; the walk explains other movements separately.**
15. **ECL and IRAC use different bases; Standard classification does not guarantee Stage 1.**
16. **Deterioration can reduce capital and raise RWA together; falling RWA can follow a damaging loss.**
17. **LCR measures stressed short-term coverage; NSFR measures structural funding support; intraday liquidity measures payment timing.**
18. **A higher LCR can coexist with less HQLA in rupees; inspect numerator, denominator and cash timing.**
19. **NII measures earnings sensitivity; EVE measures lifetime value sensitivity; neither replaces the other.**
20. **Shared behavioural evidence affects FTP, liquidity outflows and EVE; each retains its own calculation rules.**
21. **FTP prices the deposit’s funding value; NSFR weights its stability; both concern the same liability.**
22. **FTP needs the right tenor, currency, curve vintage and charging base; internal entries must cancel.**
23. **A missing field breaks a specific output; DQ controls protect that output and preserve visible exceptions.**
24. **Source, transformation and definition defects need different owners; trace the first incorrect hand-off.**
25. **Reconcile positions and explain movements; retain snapshots, rule versions, adjustments and approvals.**
26. **Define expected results before execution; explain parallel-run differences before cutover; finish with a controlled production cycle.**
