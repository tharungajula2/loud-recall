## Part 0 — The lender we follow

Every concept in this series attaches to one lender, one loan book and one question, so that each new idea lands on numbers already familiar.

A mid-sized Indian lender gives unsecured personal loans. Between January 2023 and December 2024 it disbursed **50,000 loans**. Twelve months after each disbursal it knows which borrowers stopped paying. The risk team wants a model that, at the moment someone applies, estimates the **probability that this borrower defaults within twelve months** — so the lender can approve, decline or price the loan before the money leaves.

### The book, in numbers

|---|---:|---|

| **Default rate** | **5.05%** | 2,526 ÷ 50,000 |
| Median monthly income | ₹48,800 | Declared at application |
| Median loan | ₹2.46 lakh | |

The data is synthetic, generated by code shown in full in Part 2, so every number in this volume can be reproduced exactly by running the snippets beside it. It is built to behave like a real unsecured book: bureau score matters most, heavy existing EMIs matter more than proportionally, new-to-credit borrowers are riskier, and later cohorts are slightly worse than earlier ones.

**The one fact to hold onto: 95 of every 100 borrowers repay.** That imbalance shapes almost every decision in this volume, starting with why accuracy is the wrong way to measure the model.

## Part 1 — What machine learning is, and what we are predicting

### Learning a function from examples

A program normally encodes rules someone wrote: *decline if the bureau score is below 650.* **Machine learning** reverses that. We supply examples — applications and what happened to them — and an algorithm finds a rule, a **function** from inputs to outputs, that fits those examples and, crucially, works on applications it has never seen.

| Word | Plain meaning | Here |
|---|---|---|
| **Example**, row, observation | One case | One loan |
| **Feature**, input, x | A measured property of the case, known when the prediction is made | Bureau score, income, FOIR at application |
| **Target**, label, y | What we want to predict | Did this loan default within 12 months: 1 or 0 |
| **Model** | The learned function f(x) | A formula giving a probability of default |
| **Training** | Choosing the model's numbers — its **parameters** — to fit the examples | Part 6 |
| **Generalisation** | Working on new cases, not just the ones trained on | The only thing that matters — Parts 3 and 8 |

**Memorising is not learning.** A lookup table of all 50,000 loans would be perfect on those loans and useless on the next applicant. Every safeguard in this volume exists to make sure the model has learned a pattern rather than memorised a sample.

### The kinds of learning

| Kind | What the data contains | Typical question | In this series |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Supervised** | Features and a known target | Will this applicant default? | Volumes 1 and 2 |
| — **Classification** | Target is a category | Default or not | This volume's main model |
| — **Regression** | Target is a number | What interest rate does this score get? | Part 5 |
| **Unsupervised** | Features only | Which borrowers look alike? | Volume 2 |
| **Reinforcement** | Actions and rewards over time | Which collections action works best? | Volume 3, briefly |

A **default model** — in banking, a **probability of default (PD) model** — is supervised classification. But the output wanted is not a label; it is a **probability**, because the lender's decision depends on how likely, not merely whether.

### Defining the target — the decision nobody should skip

"Did the borrower default?" is not yet a definition. Four choices make it one, and each changes the model.

| Choice | Options | This lender's decision | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What counts as default** | 30, 60 or 90+ days past due (DPD); write-off; restructuring | **90+ DPD**, or write-off | Matches the regulatory non-performing threshold and the lender's own loss experience |
| **Observation point** | When the features are measured | **The application date** | The model must predict before the money is lent |
| **Performance window** | How long we wait to see the outcome | **12 months from disbursal** | Long enough for most unsecured defaults to show; short enough that the data is recent |
| **Exclusions** | Fraud, deceased borrowers, loans closed early | Fraud excluded and modelled separately | A fraudster does not "default"; mixing them teaches the wrong pattern |

Two consequences follow. **A loan disbursed in December 2024 has an outcome only from December 2025**, so the newest data always lags by a year. And **changing the definition changes the model**: a 30-DPD target has more positives and catches earlier stress, but many 30-DPD borrowers cure and would have been profitable.

Days past due (DPD) is how many days an instalment is overdue. A borrower who misses one monthly EMI is 30 DPD; three in a row makes them 90 DPD.

**Decision, Part 1:** supervised classification; output a probability; target = 90+ DPD or write-off within 12 months of disbursal, measured from application-time features only.

## Part 2 — The data, and what is wrong with it

### The data dictionary

| Column | Meaning | Known at application? |
|---|---|---|
| `application_date` | When the customer applied | Yes |
| `age` | Years | Yes |
| `employment_type` | Salaried or self-employed | Yes |
| `city_tier` | 1 metro, 2 large city, 3 smaller town | Yes |
| `monthly_income` | Declared, ₹ | Yes |
| `bureau_score` | Credit bureau score, 300–900; missing if new to credit | Yes |
| `enquiries_6m` | Credit enquiries in the last six months | Yes |
| `existing_emi` | Monthly instalments on other loans, ₹ | Yes |
| `loan_amount`, `tenure_months`, `interest_rate`, `emi` | The loan offered | Yes |
| `months_at_employer` | Job tenure | Yes |
| `dpd_month_3` | Days past due three months after disbursal | **No — after** |
| `default_12m` | The target | No — it is the answer |

One derived feature carries the lender's affordability policy. **FOIR**, the **fixed obligations to income ratio**, is all monthly EMIs, including the new one, divided by monthly income:

$$
\text{FOIR} = \frac{\text{Existing EMI} + \text{New EMI}}{\text{Monthly income}}
$$

where:

- **Existing EMI** — instalments already being paid on other loans
- **New EMI** — the instalment on this loan
- **Monthly income** — declared income

A FOIR of 0.55 means 55% of income is already committed before rent, food or anything else.

### Looking before modelling

**Exploratory data analysis (EDA)** is reading the data before fitting anything: distributions, missing values, and the target rate across each feature. Here it is on the whole book.

| Bureau score band | Loans | Defaults | Default rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 620–679 | 7,847 | 755 | 9.62% |
| 680–719 | 10,888 | 626 | 5.75% |
| 720–759 | 12,151 | 426 | 3.51% |
| 760–799 | 9,100 | 218 | 2.40% |
| 800+ | 5,833 | 88 | 1.51% |
| **No score** | 4,181 | 413 | **9.88%** |

| FOIR band | Loans | Default rate |
|---|---:|---:|
| up to 0.30 | 15,972 | 4.18% |
| 0.30–0.40 | 14,122 | 4.37% |
| 0.40–0.50 | 10,714 | 5.17% |
| 0.50–0.60 | 6,205 | 6.72% |
| 0.60–0.70 | 2,987 | 9.04% |

| Enquiries in 6 months | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4+ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Default rate | 3.65% | 4.54% | 5.82% | 7.64% | 11.42% |

| Segment | Default rate |
|---|---:|
| Salaried | 4.51% |
| Self-employed | 6.44% |
| City tier 1 / 2 / 3 | 4.81% / 5.00% / 5.69% |

Four readings, each of which becomes a modelling decision later.

**Bureau score is the strongest single signal** — a six-fold spread from 620 to 800-plus — and it is roughly monotonic: higher is safer at every step.

**FOIR is not linear.** From 0.30 to 0.40 the default rate barely moves; from 0.60 to 0.70 it jumps. A model that assumes each extra point of FOIR adds the same risk will be wrong at the top end — Part 7.

**Missing is information.** The 8.4% with no bureau score default at 9.88%, the riskiest group in the book. Filling their score with an average and forgetting they were missing would erase that. Data missing because of what the borrower *is* — new to credit — is **missing not at random**, and must be flagged.

**Weak features still count.** City tier moves the rate by less than a point. It may add a little, or only noise; Parts 7 and 8 decide.

### The data we do not have

Every loan in the book was **approved**. The lender's policy declines anyone with FOIR above 0.70 or a bureau score below 620 (unless new to credit), so the book contains no borrowers from those regions, and the model will learn nothing about them. Using it to decide on such applicants would be extrapolation — predicting outside the range the data covers.

This is **selection bias**, and in lending it has a name: the **reject inference** problem. The applicants the model most needs to judge well — the borderline ones near the policy edge — are the ones least represented. Techniques to estimate how rejected applicants would have behaved exist and are imperfect; the honest first step is to **write down the population the model is valid for** and keep policy rules outside the model for everyone else.

Optional reading — the generator, so every number here can be reproduced. Save it as `loanbook.py`.

```python
import numpy as np, pandas as pd

def make_loan_book(n=50_000, seed=42):
    return _approved(_applications(int(n * 1.6), seed), n)

def _applications(n, seed):
    """Synthetic personal-loan book for one Indian lender, Jan 2023 - Dec 2024."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    month = rng.integers(0, 24, n)                                   # 0 = Jan 2023
    app_date = pd.to_datetime("2023-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(month * 30.4 + rng.integers(0, 28, n), unit="D")
    age = rng.integers(21, 61, n)
    employment = np.where(rng.random(n) < 0.72, "salaried", "self_employed")
    city_tier = rng.choice([1, 2, 3], n, p=[0.45, 0.35, 0.20])
    income = np.round(np.exp(rng.normal(np.log(45_000), 0.55, n)) * np.where(city_tier == 1, 1.2, 1.0), -2)
    bureau = np.clip(rng.normal(730, 60, n), 300, 900).round()
    ntc = rng.random(n) < 0.08                                       # new to credit: no bureau score
    bureau = np.where(ntc, np.nan, bureau)
    enquiries = rng.poisson(1.2, n)
    existing_emi = np.round(income * rng.beta(2, 10, n), -2)
    loan_amount = np.clip(np.round(income * rng.uniform(2, 9, n), -3), 50_000, 1_000_000)
    tenure = rng.choice([12, 24, 36, 48, 60], n, p=[0.1, 0.25, 0.35, 0.2, 0.1])
    score_for_price = np.where(ntc, 700, bureau)
    rate = np.clip(np.round(22 - (score_for_price - 650) * 0.04 + rng.normal(0, 1, n), 1), 11, 24)
    r = rate / 1200
    emi = np.round(loan_amount * r * (1 + r) ** tenure / ((1 + r) ** tenure - 1), 0)
    foir = (existing_emi + emi) / income
    months_employer = rng.integers(1, 180, n)

    z = (-3.55
         - 0.012 * (np.where(ntc, 700, bureau) - 730)
         + 0.55 * ntc
         + 2.2 * np.maximum(foir - 0.35, 0) + 3.5 * np.maximum(foir - 0.60, 0)
         + 0.28 * enquiries
         + 0.35 * (employment == "self_employed")
         + 0.15 * (city_tier == 3)
         - 0.004 * months_employer
         - 0.010 * (age - 35)
         + 0.012 * month)                                              # later cohorts slightly riskier
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))
    default = (rng.random(n) < p).astype(int)
    # a post-disbursal field that should never be a feature
    dpd_m3 = np.where(default == 1, rng.choice([0, 30, 60], n, p=[0.35, 0.40, 0.25]),
                      rng.choice([0, 30], n, p=[0.97, 0.03]))
    return pd.DataFrame({
        "loan_id": np.arange(1, n + 1), "application_date": app_date.normalize(),
        "age": age, "employment_type": employment, "city_tier": city_tier,
        "monthly_income": income, "bureau_score": bureau, "enquiries_6m": enquiries,
        "existing_emi": existing_emi, "loan_amount": loan_amount, "tenure_months": tenure,
        "interest_rate": rate, "emi": emi, "months_at_employer": months_employer,
        "dpd_month_3": dpd_m3, "default_12m": default})


def _approved(apps, n):
    foir = (apps.existing_emi + apps.emi) / apps.monthly_income
    ok = (foir <= 0.70) & ((apps.bureau_score >= 620) | apps.bureau_score.isna())
    book = apps[ok].head(n).reset_index(drop=True)
    book["loan_id"] = range(1, len(book) + 1)
    return book
```

## Part 3 — Leakage, and the split that tells the truth

### How a model cheats

**Leakage** is information in the training features that would not be available when the model is used. A leaking model looks brilliant in development and fails in production, and it is the single most common way a model is wrong without anyone noticing.

The data has a trap. `dpd_month_3` — how far behind the borrower was three months after disbursal — is in the table, it is predictive, and it is recorded **after** the decision the model is meant to make.

| Model | Test AUC |
|---|---:|
| Application features only | 0.730 |
| Application features plus `dpd_month_3` | **0.902** |

AUC is defined in Part 9; for now, 0.5 is guessing and 1.0 is perfect. The leaking model is not better. It is **answering a question nobody will ask it**: at application, month three has not happened.

| Kind of leakage | How it gets in | Here | Prevented by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Target leakage** | A feature recorded after, or because of, the outcome | `dpd_month_3`; a "collections flag" | Label every column with when it is known; use only application-time columns |
| **Temporal leakage** | Training on the future to predict the past | A random split mixing 2024 loans into training for 2023 tests | An out-of-time split |
| **Preprocessing leakage** | Scaling, imputing or encoding fitted on all the data, test included | The median bureau score computed on the whole book | Fit every transformation on training data only — Part 11's pipeline |
| **Entity leakage** | The same borrower appears in training and test | A repeat customer's two loans split across both | Split by customer, not by loan |

**Trap — "too good to be true" is a finding, not a celebration.** An unsecured PD model with an AUC above about 0.85 on application data should be investigated for leakage before anyone sees it.

### The split

A model is judged on data it did not train on. Three portions do three jobs.

| Portion | Job | Touched how often |
|---|---|---|
| **Training set** | Fit the parameters | Constantly |
| **Validation set** | Choose between models and settings | Often — and so it slowly becomes part of what was fitted |
| **Test set** | One honest estimate at the end | **Once** |

The split must mirror use. The model will be trained on the past and applied to the future, so it is tested the same way: an **out-of-time** split.

| Portion | Applications | Loans | Defaults | Default rate |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Train | Jan 2023 – Jun 2024 | 37,516 | 1,859 | 4.96% |
| Validation | Jul – Sep 2024 | 6,330 | 332 | 5.24% |
| Test | Oct – Dec 2024 | 6,154 | 335 | 5.44% |

```python
d = book.application_date
train = book[d < "2024-07-01"]
val   = book[(d >= "2024-07-01") & (d < "2024-10-01")]
test  = book[d >= "2024-10-01"]
```

The default rate drifts upward across the three periods — 4.96%, 5.24%, 5.44%. A random split would have hidden that, averaging all three into every portion. Here the random-split AUC (0.720) and the out-of-time test AUC (0.730) happen to be close, so ranking holds up; the damage shows elsewhere, in calibration, where Part 9 finds the model predicting 4.96% for a cohort that defaults at 5.44%. **The out-of-time split is not there to lower the score. It is there to show what the future will do to the model before the future does.**

**Decision, Part 3:** application-time features only; `dpd_month_3` excluded and the reason written down; out-of-time split with the test set used once; every transformation fitted on training data.

## Part 4 — Baselines, and the metric the business pays for

### The accuracy trap

Before building anything, ask what a model with no intelligence would score. The test set defaults at 5.44%. A "model" that approves everyone and predicts *no default* for every loan is right on 5,819 of 6,154 loans:

$$
\text{Accuracy of predicting nobody defaults} = 1 - 0.0544 = 0.9456
$$

where:

- **0.0544** — the test set's default rate

**94.6% accurate, and it catches not one defaulter.** On imbalanced data accuracy rewards ignoring the minority class, which is the only class the lender cares about. It is never the headline metric for a default model.

### The confusion matrix

Any rule that turns a prediction into approve or decline produces four counts. Take the simplest sensible rule a credit officer might write: **decline if the bureau score is below 680 or missing.** On the test set:

| | Actually defaulted | Actually repaid |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Declined — predicted bad** | **True positive** 177 | **False positive** 1,321 |
| **Approved — predicted good** | **False negative** 158 | **True negative** 4,498 |

"Positive" means the event being hunted — a default. Every useful classification metric is a ratio of these four cells.

| Metric | Formula | Question it answers | The 680 rule |
|---|---|---|---:|
| **Accuracy** | (TP + TN) ÷ all | What share was classified right? | 0.760 |
| **Recall**, sensitivity, true positive rate | TP ÷ (TP + FN) | Of the defaulters, what share did we decline? | **0.528** |
| **Precision** | TP ÷ (TP + FP) | Of the declined, what share would have defaulted? | 0.118 |
| **False positive rate** | FP ÷ (FP + TN) | Of the good borrowers, what share did we turn away? | 0.227 |
| **Specificity** | TN ÷ (FP + TN) | Of the good borrowers, what share did we keep? | 0.773 |
| **F1** | 2 × P × R ÷ (P + R) | One number balancing precision and recall | 0.193 |

The rule catches half the defaulters, and to do it declines 1,321 good borrowers — **7.5 good customers turned away for every defaulter stopped.** Whether that is a good trade is not a statistical question.

### The metric the business pays for

The lender earns a margin on every good loan and loses most of the balance on every bad one. Part 10 prices both. Previewing its result: the 680 rule lowers the bad rate among approved loans from 5.44% to 3.39% — and **makes less money than approving everyone**, ₹10.76 crore against ₹11.54 crore on the test quarter, because the good borrowers it turns away were worth more than the defaults it avoids.

So three levels of metric are needed, and they answer different questions.

| Level | Metric | Answers | Part |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ranking** | AUC, Gini, KS | Does the model put riskier borrowers above safer ones? Independent of any cut-off | 9 |
| **Probability** | Calibration, Brier score, log loss | When it says 8%, do 8% default? | 9 |
| **Decision** | Profit, approval rate, bad rate at a cut-off | What does using it earn? | 10 |

**Decision, Part 4:** accuracy is not reported; the model is judged on ranking and calibration, and the decision rule on profit. The 680 rule is the **baseline** every model must beat — on profit, not on accuracy.

## Part 5 — Linear regression, from nothing

Before a default model, the simplest learner of all, on a question the lender also has: **how does the interest rate it charges depend on the bureau score?** The answer is a line, and every idea in Part 6 is this Part with one change.

### The model

$$
\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x
$$

where:

- **ŷ** — the predicted interest rate, "y hat"
- **x** — the bureau score
- **β₀** — the intercept: the prediction when x is zero
- **β₁** — the slope: how much the rate changes per point of score

Learning means choosing β₀ and β₁. The standard choice, **ordinary least squares (OLS)**, picks the line that minimises the sum of squared errors — the **residuals** — between each actual and predicted value.

### By hand, on five loans

Take five loans. Write the score as hundreds of points above 600 — so 650 is 0.5 — to keep the arithmetic small.

| Loan | Score | x | Rate y, % |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 650 | 0.5 | 21.0 |
| 2 | 700 | 1.0 | 19.5 |
| 3 | 750 | 1.5 | 19.0 |
| 4 | 800 | 2.0 | 17.0 |
| 5 | 850 | 2.5 | 16.5 |

The least-squares slope and intercept have closed forms:

$$
\beta_1 = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \qquad \beta_0 = \bar{y} - \beta_1 \bar{x}
$$

where:

- **x̄, ȳ** — the means of x and y
- **Σ** — the sum over the five loans

| Step | Working | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Means | x̄ = 7.5 ÷ 5; ȳ = 93 ÷ 5 | 1.5; 18.6 |
| Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) | (−1)(2.4) + (−0.5)(0.9) + 0 + (0.5)(−1.6) + (1)(−2.1) | −5.75 |
| Σ(x − x̄)² | 1 + 0.25 + 0 + 0.25 + 1 | 2.5 |
| **β₁** | −5.75 ÷ 2.5 | **−2.3** |
| **β₀** | 18.6 − (−2.3)(1.5) | **22.05** |

**Each 100 points of score takes 2.3 percentage points off the rate**, from 22.05% at a score of 600.

How good is the line? Compare its errors with the errors of simply predicting the average.

| Loan | Actual | Predicted | Residual |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 21.0 | 20.90 | 0.10 |
| 2 | 19.5 | 19.75 | −0.25 |
| 3 | 19.0 | 18.60 | 0.40 |
| 4 | 17.0 | 17.45 | −0.45 |
| 5 | 16.5 | 16.30 | 0.20 |

$$
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \frac{0.475}{13.7} = 0.965
$$

where:

- **SSE** — sum of squared residuals, 0.01 + 0.0625 + 0.16 + 0.2025 + 0.04 = 0.475
- **SST** — sum of squared deviations from the mean, 5.76 + 0.81 + 0.16 + 2.56 + 4.41 = 13.7
- **R²** — the share of the variation in y the line explains; 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than the mean

### On the whole book, with matrices

With many features, the same least-squares problem is written with a matrix **X** — one row per loan, a column of ones for the intercept, one column per feature — and solved in one step, the **normal equation**:

$$
\boldsymbol{\beta} = (X^{\top}X)^{-1} X^{\top} y
$$

where:

- **X** — the n × (p + 1) feature matrix, first column all ones
- **y** — the n targets
- **β** — the p + 1 coefficients

In code, the inverse is never computed directly — it is numerically fragile — and a least-squares solver is used instead.

```python
import numpy as np
scored = train.dropna(subset=["bureau_score"])
X = np.c_[np.ones(len(scored)), scored.bureau_score]
y = scored.interest_rate.values
beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)      # [47.91, -0.03988]
resid = y - X @ beta

```

### Gradient descent — learning by small steps

The normal equation works for linear regression and nothing else. Almost every other model in this series is trained by **gradient descent**: start with any parameters, measure which direction reduces the error, step a little that way, repeat. The error to reduce is the **mean squared error (MSE)**, and its slope with respect to the parameters — its **gradient** — is:

$$
\nabla_{\beta}\,\text{MSE} = \frac{2}{n} X^{\top}(X\beta - y)
$$

where:

- **∇β MSE** — the vector of partial derivatives of the error with respect to each coefficient
- **Xβ − y** — the residuals, predicted minus actual
- **n** — the number of loans

Each step moves the coefficients against the gradient by a **learning rate** η:

$$
\beta \leftarrow \beta - \eta\, \nabla_{\beta}\,\text{MSE}
$$

where:

- **η** — the learning rate, 0.1 here; too small is slow, too large overshoots and diverges

Run it with the score **standardised** — rescaled to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 — starting from zero:

| Step | Intercept | Slope per SD of score | MSE |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 3.72 | −0.44 | 225.6 |
| 5 | 12.51 | −1.48 | 38.68 |
| 20 | 18.39 | −2.18 | 1.040 |
| 50 | 18.60 | −2.20 | 0.993 |
| 200 | **18.60** | **−2.20** | **0.993** |

The slope per standard deviation, −2.204, divided by the score's standard deviation gives −0.03988 per point — **the same answer the normal equation gave**, reached by walking downhill. The intercept settles at 18.60, the average rate, because a standardised feature has mean zero.

```python
z = (scored.bureau_score - scored.bureau_score.mean()) / scored.bureau_score.std(ddof=0)
Xz, w = np.c_[np.ones(len(z)), z], np.zeros(2)
for step in range(200):
    w -= 0.1 * 2 * Xz.T @ (Xz @ w - y) / len(y)
```

Why standardise? Unstandardised, the score is in the hundreds and the intercept column is 1, so a learning rate small enough for one is useless for the other. Part 7 shows the logistic model diverging for exactly this reason.

| Gradient descent variant | Each step uses | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Batch** | All rows | Smooth, slow per step |
| **Stochastic** | One row | Noisy, fast, can escape shallow traps |
| **Mini-batch** | A few hundred rows | The default everywhere, including Volume 3's neural networks |

### Checking a regression

| Assumption | Meaning | Check | Here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linearity | The relationship is a straight line | Residuals against fitted values show no curve | Holds — the grid is linear by design |
| Independence | Errors are unrelated to each other | No pattern over time | Holds |
| Constant variance | Errors are equally spread at all x — homoscedasticity | Residual spread does not fan out | Holds |
| Normal errors | Matters for confidence intervals, not for predictions | Residual histogram | Roughly |
| No multicollinearity | Features are not near-copies of each other | **Variance inflation factor (VIF)** below about 5–10 | Only one feature |

| Regression metric | Formula | Reads as |
|---|---|---|
| MSE | mean of squared errors | Penalises big misses heavily; in squared units |
| **RMSE** | √MSE | Typical error in the target's own units — here percentage points |
| MAE | mean of absolute errors | Typical error, less swayed by outliers |
| R² | 1 − SSE ÷ SST | Share of variation explained |

### Why a line cannot predict default

Fit the same least-squares line to the 0-or-1 default flag against bureau score — a **linear probability model**:

| Score | Predicted "probability" of default |
|---:|---:|
| 620 | 10.4% |
| 750 | 4.2% |
| **900** | **−2.97%** |

A negative probability. A straight line is unbounded; a probability must lie between 0 and 1. And the effect of score on risk is not constant — moving from 620 to 680 matters far more than from 840 to 900. Both problems have one fix.

**Decision, Part 5:** linear regression for numeric targets such as the pricing grid; gradient descent as the general training method; standardise features before any gradient-based fit; never use a line for a probability.

## Part 6 — Logistic regression, from nothing

### Squashing a line into a probability

Keep the linear combination from Part 5, and pass it through a function that maps any number into the range 0 to 1 — the **sigmoid**, or logistic function:

$$
p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \qquad z = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_k x_k
$$

where:

- **z** — the linear score, any real number
- **p** — the predicted probability of default
- **e** — the base of natural logarithms, 2.718…

| z | −4 | −3 | −2 | 0 | 2 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| p | 1.8% | 4.7% | 11.9% | 50% | 88.1% |

Turn it round and the meaning of z appears. The **odds** of default are p ÷ (1 − p) — at p = 5%, odds of 1 to 19. The logarithm of the odds, the **logit**, is exactly the linear score:

$$
\ln\!\left(\frac{p}{1 - p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_k x_k
$$

where:

- **p ÷ (1 − p)** — the odds of default
- **ln** — the natural logarithm

So each coefficient is **the change in log-odds per unit of its feature**, and e raised to it is an **odds ratio** — the factor by which the odds multiply. This is why logistic regression has been the backbone of credit scoring for decades: every coefficient reads as a sentence.

### What "best" means: log loss

Squared error is the wrong yardstick for probabilities. The right one asks: under the model's probabilities, how likely was what actually happened? Maximising that likelihood is the same as minimising the **log loss**, also called binary cross-entropy:

$$
\text{Log loss} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y_i \ln p_i + (1 - y_i)\ln(1 - p_i)\Big]
$$

where:

- **yᵢ** — 1 if loan i defaulted, else 0
- **pᵢ** — the model's probability for loan i

For a defaulter only ln pᵢ counts; for a good loan only ln(1 − pᵢ). Saying 1% for a loan that defaulted costs −ln 0.01 = 4.6; saying 30% costs 1.2. **Confident mistakes are punished hardest.**

Its gradient has the same shape as linear regression's — prediction minus actual, times the features:

$$
\nabla_{\beta}\,\text{Log loss} = \frac{1}{n} X^{\top}(p - y)
$$

where:

- **p − y** — each loan's predicted probability minus its outcome

There is no closed-form solution, so gradient descent does the work. The log loss of a logistic model is **convex** — one bowl, no false bottoms — so gradient descent finds the single best answer from any start.

### Built from scratch

Eight features, standardised on the training set: bureau score (with the training median, 733, filled in where missing), a no-score flag, FOIR, enquiries, self-employed, city tier 3, months at employer and age.

```python
def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

def fit_logistic(X, y, lr=0.5, steps=3000):
    X = np.c_[np.ones(len(X)), X]              # intercept column
    w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = sigmoid(X @ w)
        w -= lr * X.T @ (p - y) / len(y)       # the gradient above
    return w
```

| Step | Training log loss |
|---:|---:|
| 0 — all coefficients zero, every p = 0.5 | 0.6931 |
| 1 | 0.5974 |
| 10 | 0.2885 |
| 100 | 0.1840 |
| 1,000 | 0.18208 |
| 3,000 | **0.18208** — converged |

## Part 7 — Features that carry the signal

A model can only use what its features express. **Feature engineering** is shaping raw columns so the signal in them is reachable. For a linear model it matters more than for anything in Volume 2, because a linear model cannot invent a curve or an interaction on its own.

### Scaling

Run Part 6's gradient descent on the raw, unscaled features — bureau score in the hundreds, months at employer up to 180, flags of 0 or 1 — with the same learning rate of 0.5:

| Features | Training log loss after 200 steps |
|---|---:|
| Standardised | ~0.183 |
| **Raw** | **32.8** — diverged |

One learning rate cannot suit a feature whose values are near 700 and one whose values are near 0.3: the step that is small for one is enormous for the other, and the loss explodes. **Standardise** — subtract the training mean, divide by the training standard deviation — before any gradient-based fit, and before any penalty in Part 8, which would otherwise punish features for their units rather than their usefulness.

| Transformation | Formula | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Standardisation** | (x − mean) ÷ SD | The default for linear models and neural networks |
| **Min–max scaling** | (x − min) ÷ (max − min) | When a fixed range is needed |
| **Log transform** | ln(x) | Skewed money amounts such as income |
| None | | Tree models in Volume 2, which only compare values against thresholds |

Every statistic — mean, SD, median — is **computed on the training set only** and then applied unchanged to validation and test. Computing them on everything is preprocessing leakage from Part 3.

### Categories

| Encoding | How | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **One-hot** | One 0/1 column per category, one dropped as the reference | Employment type, city tier — no natural order |
| **Ordinal** | Categories mapped to ordered integers | Only when the order is real and the steps roughly equal |
| **Target encoding** | Each category replaced by its default rate | High-cardinality columns such as pin code; must be computed out-of-fold or it leaks the target |

City tier looks numeric — 1, 2, 3 — but treating it as a number forces tier 3 to be exactly as far from tier 2 as tier 2 is from tier 1. One-hot encoding lets the data decide.

### Missing values

| Approach | Effect here |
|---|---|
| Drop rows with missing score | Throws away the riskiest 8.4% of the book |
| Fill with the median | Makes a new-to-credit borrower look like an average one |
| **Fill with the median and add a missing flag** | Keeps the rows, and the flag's coefficient — odds × 2.56 in Part 6 — carries the risk of being unscored |

The third is what Part 6 did, and what Part 11's pipeline does automatically.

### Weight of evidence and information value

Credit scoring has its own feature tool, older than most of machine learning and still standard in banks. Bin a feature, then replace each bin by its **weight of evidence (WoE)**:

$$
\text{WoE}_{\text{bin}} = \ln\!\left(\frac{\%\ \text{of all goods in the bin}}{\%\ \text{of all bads in the bin}}\right)
$$

where:

- **% of all goods in the bin** — the bin's non-defaulters as a share of all non-defaulters
- **% of all bads in the bin** — the bin's defaulters as a share of all defaulters

A positive WoE means the bin holds relatively more good borrowers. Summing each bin's contribution gives the feature's **information value (IV)**, a measure of how well it separates goods from bads:

$$
\text{IV} = \sum_{\text{bins}} \left(\%\ \text{goods} - \%\ \text{bads}\right) \times \text{WoE}
$$

where:

- **the sum** — runs over the feature's bins

On the training set — 35,657 goods, 1,859 bads:

| Bureau band | Bads | Goods | % goods | % bads | WoE | IV contribution |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 620–679 | 561 | 5,318 | 14.91% | 30.18% | −0.705 | 0.108 |
| 680–719 | 472 | 7,694 | 21.58% | 25.39% | −0.163 | 0.006 |
| 720–759 | 310 | 8,833 | 24.77% | 16.68% | +0.396 | 0.032 |
| 760–799 | 168 | 6,658 | 18.67% | 9.04% | +0.726 | 0.070 |
| 800+ | 61 | 4,328 | 12.14% | 3.28% | +1.308 | 0.116 |
| No score | 287 | 2,826 | 7.93% | 15.44% | −0.667 | 0.050 |
| **Total** | 1,859 | 35,657 | | | | **0.382** |

| Feature | IV | Conventional reading |
|---|---:|---|
| Bureau score band | 0.382 | Strong |
| Enquiries, capped at 4 | 0.100 | Medium |
| FOIR band | 0.065 | Weak |
| Employment type | 0.031 | Weak |
| Age band | 0.014 | Negligible |
| City tier | 0.005 | Negligible |

The readings follow an industry rule of thumb — below 0.02 useless, 0.02 to 0.1 weak, 0.1 to 0.3 medium, above 0.3 strong — which is a convention, not a law. Two things WoE does well: it makes the relationship with log-odds straight by construction, so a linear model fits it exactly; and it handles missing as just another bin. A traditional **scorecard** is a logistic regression on WoE-binned features.

```python
def woe_table(frame, bins, target="default_12m"):
    t = frame.groupby(bins, observed=True)[target].agg(bad="sum", n="size")
    t["good"] = t.n - t.bad
    pg, pb = t.good / t.good.sum(), t.bad / t.bad.sum()
    t["woe"] = np.log(pg / pb)
    t["iv"] = (pg - pb) * t.woe
    return t, t.iv.sum()
```

### Non-linearity: FOIR

Part 2 showed FOIR's risk accelerating above 0.5. A linear term cannot bend, so add a **hinge** — a feature that is zero below a knot and grows above it:

$$
\text{FOIR above 0.5} = \max(\text{FOIR} - 0.5,\ 0)
$$

where:

- **0.5** — the knot, chosen where Part 2's table starts to climb

| Model | Validation AUC | Validation log loss | Test AUC |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Part 6's eight features | 0.7113 | 0.19104 | 0.7304 |
| Plus the FOIR hinge | 0.7127 | 0.19091 | 0.7317 |

A small, consistent gain. Small because only 18% of loans have FOIR above 0.5; real, because it is where the policy edge sits. **Interactions** — a feature multiplied by another, such as FOIR × self-employed — are built the same way, and a linear model gets them only if someone writes them. Volume 2's trees find both kinds of shape on their own, which is much of their advantage.

**Decision, Part 7:** standardise on training statistics; one-hot the categories; median plus missing flag for bureau score; add the FOIR hinge; keep a WoE-binned scorecard as the documented alternative. City tier and age are weak, and Part 8 decides whether they stay.

## Part 8 — Overfitting, regularisation and cross-validation

### Two ways to be wrong

A model's error on new data has two avoidable sources.

| | **Bias** — underfitting | **Variance** — overfitting |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | The model is too simple to express the pattern | The model fits noise peculiar to its training sample |
| Symptom | Poor on training and validation alike | Good on training, much worse on validation |
| Cure | More expressive model, better features | More data, fewer features, **regularisation** |

A **learning curve** shows which one is present: fit on growing samples of the training set and score each on the same validation set.

| Training loans | Train AUC | Validation AUC | Gap |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 500 | 0.745 | 0.649 | 0.096 |
| 1,000 | 0.736 | 0.677 | 0.059 |
| 2,000 | 0.736 | 0.702 | 0.034 |
| 5,000 | 0.734 | 0.709 | 0.025 |
| 10,000 | 0.737 | 0.711 | 0.026 |
| 20,000 | 0.719 | 0.711 | 0.008 |
| 37,516 | 0.719 | 0.711 | 0.008 |

At 500 loans — about 25 defaults — the model memorises and the gap is 0.10. By 20,000 the curves have met, and more data no longer helps. **A model whose curves have met is limited by bias**: to go higher it needs better features or a more flexible form, which is the argument for Volume 2.

### Regularisation

**Regularisation** adds a penalty on the size of the coefficients to the loss, so the model only uses a feature strongly if the data insists.

$$
\text{Loss} = \text{Log loss} + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{k} \beta_j^2 \quad \text{(L2, ridge)} \qquad \text{Loss} = \text{Log loss} + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{k} \lvert \beta_j \rvert \quad \text{(L1, lasso)}
$$

where:

- **λ** — the penalty strength; in scikit-learn it is set as C = 1 ÷ λ, so **smaller C means stronger regularisation**
- **β_j** — each coefficient except the intercept, which is not penalised

To see it work, create the conditions where it is needed: a small sample of 1,500 training loans, only 77 of them defaults, with the eight real features plus **thirty columns of pure random noise**.

| L2, C | Train AUC | Validation AUC | Validation log loss | Sum of \|coefficients\| |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1,000 — almost none | 0.797 | 0.649 | 0.2113 | 6.04 |
| 0.01 | 0.794 | 0.647 | **0.1984** | 2.17 |
| 0.003 | 0.793 | 0.647 | 0.2011 | 0.91 |
| 0.001 | 0.793 | 0.647 | 0.2037 | 0.35 |

**L2 barely changes the ranking and substantially improves the probabilities.** Shrinking every coefficient towards zero stops the model being over-confident — log loss falls from 0.211 to 0.198 — but it never removes a feature, so the thirty noise columns still blur the ranking. Pushed too far, it under-fits and log loss climbs again.

| L1, C | Validation AUC | Non-zero coefficients | Noise columns kept | Real features kept |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 1,000 | 0.649 | 38 | 30 | all eight |
| 0.2 | 0.665 | 26 | 20 | six |
| 0.1 | 0.678 | 15 | 10 | five |
| **0.07** | **0.680** | 7 | 4 | bureau score, no score, enquiries |
| 0.05 | 0.676 | 3 | **0** | bureau score, no score, enquiries |
| 0.03 | 0.630 | 1 | 0 | bureau score |

**L1 sets coefficients exactly to zero**, so it selects features. As the penalty tightens it discards noise first and weak real features next, and keeps the three strongest — the same three that Part 7's information values and Part 6's per-SD coefficients ranked highest. Too tight, and it throws away real signal.

| | L2 | L1 | Elastic net — both |
|---|---|---|---|
| Effect | Shrinks all coefficients | Zeroes some | Zeroes some, shrinks the rest |
| Best when | Many features, all somewhat useful | Some features are useless | Correlated groups of useful features |
| Here | Calibration on small samples | Feature selection | A reasonable default when unsure |

On the full 37,516-loan training set with eight sensible features, regularisation changes almost nothing: the learning curve already shows no overfitting. **Regularisation is insurance whose value rises as data per feature falls.**

```python
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
ridge = LogisticRegression(C=0.01, max_iter=5000)                           # L2 is the default
lasso = LogisticRegression(C=0.07, l1_ratio=1.0, solver="liblinear")        # L1
```

### Cross-validation — choosing without wasting the test set

Choosing C by trying values against the validation set works, but a single validation split is itself a sample, and choosing on it repeatedly slowly fits it. **k-fold cross-validation** splits the training set into k parts, trains on k − 1 and scores on the one left out, k times, and averages.

| Fold | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | Mean | SD |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Validation AUC | 0.730 | 0.695 | 0.715 | 0.715 | 0.732 | **0.717** | **0.013** |

The spread matters as much as the mean: two models whose AUCs differ by less than about 0.013 on this data cannot be told apart by one split.

| Variant | How | When |
|---|---|---|
| **k-fold** | Random equal parts | Plenty of data, no time order |
| **Stratified k-fold** | Each fold keeps the 5% default rate | Imbalanced targets — always here |
| **Time-series, rolling** | Train on earlier months, validate on the next | When drift matters; it mirrors Part 3's out-of-time split |
| **Group k-fold** | All loans of one customer in the same fold | Repeat customers — Part 3's entity leakage |
| **Nested** | An inner loop chooses settings, an outer loop scores the whole procedure | Honest estimates when tuning heavily — Volume 2 |

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
scores = cross_val_score(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000)),
                         X_train, y_train, scoring="roc_auc",
                         cv=StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0))
```

The scaler sits **inside** the pipeline, so each fold's scaler is fitted on that fold's training portion only. A scaler fitted once on all of `X_train` before cross-validation would leak each fold's validation statistics into its training.

**Decision, Part 8:** the full-data model shows no overfitting; keep a mild L2 penalty as insurance; keep all eight features plus the hinge, since L1 on full data retains them; choose settings by stratified cross-validation on training data; report AUC with its fold spread.

## Part 9 — Evaluation, done properly

All figures below are on the **test set**, used once: 6,154 loans from October to December 2024, 335 defaults.

### Ranking: AUC, Gini, KS

Sweep the decision threshold from 1 down to 0. At each value, plot the **true positive rate** — share of defaulters declined — against the **false positive rate** — share of good borrowers declined. That curve is the **ROC curve**, and the **area under it (AUC)** summarises ranking in one number with a plain meaning:

> **AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen defaulter is scored riskier than a randomly chosen good borrower.**

| Metric | Formula | Test value | Reading |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **AUC** | Area under the ROC curve | **0.730** | 73% of defaulter–good pairs are ranked the right way round |

| **KS** | Largest gap between the two cumulative distributions | **0.345** | At the best single cut-off, a PD of 4.6%, the two groups are 34.5 points apart |
| PR-AUC | Area under the precision–recall curve | 0.143 | Against 0.054 for guessing; useful when positives are very rare |

| 10 | 0.83% | 0.81% | 5 | 100% |

**The riskiest tenth holds 30% of all defaults; the riskiest three-tenths hold 61%.** The riskiest decile defaults at twenty times the rate of the safest. This table — the **gains table** — is what a credit committee reads, because it translates ranking directly into "decline this many, avoid that many".

### Probabilities: calibration

Ranking is not enough. Part 10 multiplies probabilities by money, so a PD must mean what it says. A model is **calibrated** if, among loans it scores at 8%, about 8% default.

| Measure | Test value | Reading |
|---|---:|---|
| Mean predicted PD | 4.96% | |
| Actual default rate | **5.44%** | The model under-predicts by about a tenth |
| **Brier score** — mean squared error of the probabilities | 0.04936 | Against 0.05147 for predicting 5.44% for everyone: 4.1% better |
| Log loss | 0.1937 | |

The decile table shows the shape: the model is well calibrated in the middle and **under-predicts the riskiest deciles** — 14.45% predicted against 16.40% actual. The cause is Part 3's drift. The model learned from cohorts defaulting at 4.96%; the test cohort defaults at 5.44%. Ranking survived the drift; the level did not.

The standard repair is **recalibration**: keep the coefficients, shift the intercept so the average prediction matches the recent default rate.

$$
\Delta\beta_0 = \text{logit}(0.0544) - \text{logit}(0.0496) = 0.0974
$$

where:

- **logit(p)** — ln(p ÷ (1 − p))
- **0.0544, 0.0496** — the recent actual and the model's average predicted rate

In practice the shift is estimated on the most recent **matured** cohort — loans old enough to have their twelve-month outcome — and not on the test set, which stays untouched. More flexible recalibration methods — Platt scaling, isotonic regression — are in Volume 2.

### Thresholds

A threshold turns probabilities into decisions, and the default of 0.5 is meaningless here.

| Decline if PD ≥ | Declined | Defaulters caught — recall | Precision |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.50 | 0% | 0% | — |
| 0.10 | 10.8% | 31.9% | 16.0% |
| 0.068 | 24.3% | 56.1% | 12.6% |

At 0.5 the model declines nobody — no loan in the book reaches a 50% PD. At a threshold declining the same 24.3% as the 680 rule, the model catches **56.1%** of defaulters against the rule's **52.8%**, with fewer good borrowers turned away. Better — but Part 4 already showed that declining a quarter of applicants loses money. The threshold should come from economics, not from a statistic.

**Decision, Part 9:** report Gini with its cross-validation spread, KS, the gains table and calibration by decile; recalibrate the intercept on the latest matured cohort before use; choose the threshold in Part 10.

## Part 10 — From probability to decision

### The economics of one loan

| Quantity | Assumption | Per ₹100 lent |
|---|---|---:|
| Margin earned on a good loan over its life, after funding and operating cost | 10% | +₹10 |
| **Exposure at default (EAD)** — share of the loan still owed when it defaults | 85% | |
| **Loss given default (LGD)** — share of that exposure never recovered | 65% | |
| Loss on a bad loan | 0.85 × 0.65 | −₹55.25 |

Illustrative; the method is the point.

This is the credit-risk identity every bank uses: **expected loss = PD × LGD × EAD**. Approve a loan when its expected margin exceeds its expected loss:

$$
(1 - \text{PD}) \times 0.10 > \text{PD} \times 0.5525 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{PD} < \frac{0.10}{0.10 + 0.5525} = 15.33\%
$$

where:

- **0.10** — the margin on a good loan, as a share of the amount
- **0.5525** — the loss on a bad loan, LGD × EAD

**Break-even PD is 15.3%.** A borrower who defaults one time in eight is still worth lending to, because each good loan earns while each bad one loses — five and a half times as much, not twenty.

### What each policy earns

Profit on the test quarter's 6,154 loans, ₹185 crore lent, each loan's margin or loss weighted by its amount:

| Policy | Approved | Bad rate among approved | Defaults declined | Goods declined | Profit, ₹ lakh |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Approve everyone | 100% | 5.44% | 0 | 0 | 1,154.3 |
| 680 rule | 75.7% | 3.39% | 177 | 1,321 | 1,075.7 |
| Model, same decline rate as the rule | 75.7% | 3.16% | 188 | 1,310 | 1,079.7 |
| Model, break-even PD 15.3% | 97.1% | 5.09% | 31 | 148 | 1,169.6 |
| **Model, best cut-off, PD 12.8%** | **94.5%** | **4.68%** | **63** | **273** | **1,193.1** |
| Model, PD 8% | 82.2% | 3.58% | 154 | 940 | 1,145.3 |

Three readings.

**The lowest bad rate is not the most profitable book.** The 680 rule and its model twin cut the bad rate by a third and earn less than approving everyone, because every declined good loan was worth ₹10 per ₹100 lent.

**The best cut-off earns ₹38.8 lakh more than approving everyone in one quarter** — about ₹1.5 crore a year — by declining just 5.5% of applicants, the ones the model is most sure about.

**The empirical best, 12.8%, sits below the theoretical 15.3%.** Part 9 found the model under-predicting on this cohort; an applicant it scores at 13% is really riskier. Recalibrate first, and the two converge. Uncalibrated probabilities put the cut-off in the wrong place.

The cut-off itself must be chosen on validation or recent matured data and then fixed; this table uses the test set once, to report.

### The scorecard

Branches, policy documents and regulators prefer a **score** to a probability: a number where higher is safer and fixed steps mean fixed changes in odds. The convention sets a score at a chosen odds and a number of **points to double the odds (PDO)**:

$$
\text{Score} = \text{Offset} + \text{Factor} \times \ln(\text{odds of repaying}) \qquad \text{Factor} = \frac{\text{PDO}}{\ln 2}
$$

where:

- **odds of repaying** — (1 − PD) ÷ PD
- **PDO** — 20 here: every 20 points doubles the odds of repaying
- **Offset** — set so a score of 600 means odds of 50 to 1

| Step | Working | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Factor | 20 ÷ ln 2 | 28.85 |
| Offset | 600 − 28.85 × ln 50 | 487.1 |

| PD | Odds of repaying | Score |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 2% | 49 : 1 | 599 |
| 5% | 19 : 1 | 572 |
| 10% | 9 : 1 | 551 |
| 12.8% — the profit cut-off | 6.8 : 1 | 543 |
| 15.3% — break-even | 5.5 : 1 | 536 |

**Policy, stated the way a lender writes it: approve at 543 and above.** Because the score is a linear function of the log-odds, and the log-odds are Part 6's linear formula, every feature's contribution can be printed as points — the familiar scorecard table where 790 on the bureau adds so many points and three enquiries subtract so many.

**Decision, Part 10:** approve below a PD of 12.8% — score 543 — on the recalibrated model, reviewed quarterly; publish the scorecard; keep the policy rules of Part 2 outside the model.

## Part 11 — The pipeline as code

Every decision above — which columns, how they are filled, scaled and encoded, which model and settings — must be one object that is fitted once on training data and applied identically everywhere else. Otherwise production quietly computes something different from what was validated.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder, StandardScaler

numeric = ["bureau_score", "foir", "enquiries_6m", "months_at_employer", "age"]
categorical = ["employment_type", "city_tier"]

prep = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", Pipeline([("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="median", add_indicator=True)),
                      ("scale", StandardScaler())]), numeric),
    ("cat", OneHotEncoder(drop="first", handle_unknown="ignore"), categorical)])

model = Pipeline([("prep", prep), ("clf", LogisticRegression(C=1.0, max_iter=5000))])
model.fit(train[numeric + categorical], train.default_12m)

for name, part in [("val", val), ("test", test)]:
    p = model.predict_proba(part[numeric + categorical])[:, 1]
    print(name, round(roc_auc_score(part.default_12m, p), 4))      # val 0.7114, test 0.7305
```

| Pipeline piece | Decision it encodes | Part |
|---|---|---|
| `SimpleImputer(strategy="median", add_indicator=True)` | Median fill plus a missing flag for bureau score | 7 |
| `StandardScaler()` | Standardise on training statistics only | 7 |
| `OneHotEncoder(drop="first")` | Employment and city tier as categories | 7 |
| `handle_unknown="ignore"` | A new category in production does not crash scoring | — |
| `LogisticRegression(C=1.0)` | The champion, with a mild L2 penalty as insurance | 6, 8 |
| `.fit()` on `train` only | No preprocessing leakage | 3 |

City tier is one-hot here, so tier 2 gets its own column; that is why the AUCs differ from Part 6's in the fourth decimal. Adding the FOIR hinge is one more transformer; adding a penalty is one argument.

## The compression

Machine learning is choosing a function from examples so that it works on cases it has not seen. For a lender that means a probability of default at the moment of application, and everything starts with defining that target exactly — which event, from when, over what window — because a different definition is a different model.

The data is read before it is modelled. Bureau score separates risk six-fold; FOIR's risk accelerates at the top; the unscored are the riskiest group, so missing is flagged, never erased; and the book holds only approved loans, so the model is valid only inside the policy that produced it. A feature known only after the decision — three months of arrears — makes a spectacular model that answers a question nobody will ask; every column is labelled with when it is known, and the test set lies later in time than the training set, because that is how the model will be used. The out-of-time test showed ranking holding and the level drifting.

Accuracy is meaningless when 95 in 100 repay. A model is judged on ranking, on probabilities, and on the money its decisions make, against a baseline rule.

A line is learned by least squares — by hand, by the normal equation, or by gradient descent walking down the slope of the error — and cannot be a probability. Pass the same linear score through a sigmoid and it becomes one; the score is the log-odds, every coefficient an odds ratio, and log loss, convex and punishing confident mistakes, is minimised by the same descent. Written from scratch it matches the library to three decimals.

Features decide what a linear model can see: standardise on training statistics, one-hot categories, flag missing values, bin into weights of evidence, and write non-linearities such as the FOIR hinge by hand. Learning curves say whether the model is limited by variance or by bias; regularisation is insurance whose value rises as data per feature falls — L2 calming over-confident probabilities, L1 discarding noise and keeping the strongest features; cross-validation chooses settings and reports the spread that says which differences are real.

On the untouched test quarter the model ranks with an AUC of 0.73, puts 30% of defaulters in its riskiest tenth, and under-predicts a riskier cohort until its intercept is shifted. Then the probability becomes a decision: margin against PD × LGD × EAD gives a break-even PD, and profit on real amounts picks a cut-off of 12.8% — score 543 — that declines one applicant in eighteen and earns more than both approving everyone and the stricter rule with the lower bad rate. The whole chain is one pipeline object, pinned with its data, seeds, code and library versions, and explained by a decision log.

Volume 2 asks whether trees, forests and boosting — which find curves and interactions without being told — can beat this champion on the same split and the same profit table, and what it costs in explanation to let them.
